1. Central Dogma in Molecular Biology

The Central Dogma of molecular biology describes the flow of genetic information within a biological system. It was first articulated by Francis Crick in 1958.

DNA → RNA → Protein

The fundamental principle of genetic information flow

The process consists of two main stages:

  • Transcription: DNA is copied to RNA by RNA polymerase
  • Translation: RNA is used to synthesize proteins by ribosomes

Key Points:

• Information flows from DNA to RNA to protein, but not in reverse

• RNA serves as an intermediary between DNA and protein

• Some exceptions exist (reverse transcription in retroviruses)

2. Coding/Sense & Non-coding/Antisense Strands

During transcription, only one DNA strand serves as the template for RNA synthesis.

Coding Strand (Sense Strand)

  • Has the same sequence as the mRNA (except T instead of U)
  • Also called the "non-template" strand
  • Contains the genetic code for protein synthesis

Non-coding Strand (Antisense Strand)

  • Serves as the template for RNA synthesis
  • Complementary to both the coding strand and the mRNA
  • Also called the "template" strand

3. General Features of a Promoter

A promoter is a DNA sequence where RNA polymerase binds to initiate transcription.

General Promoter Features

  • Located upstream of the transcription start site
  • Contains specific DNA sequences recognized by RNA polymerase
  • Determines the exact position where transcription begins
  • Controls the efficiency and rate of transcription initiation
  • May contain binding sites for regulatory proteins
  • Strength of promoter affects transcription frequency
  • Can be constitutive (always active) or regulated (conditionally active)

Polymerase Recognition

• Sigma factors in bacteria help polymerase recognize promoters

• Transcription factors assist polymerase binding in eukaryotes

• Promoter strength affects binding efficiency

4. Upstream and Downstream Sequences and Their Annotation

DNA sequences are annotated relative to the transcription start site (+1).

Upstream Sequences

  • Located before the transcription start site (negative numbers)
  • Contain regulatory elements like promoters and enhancers
  • Example: -10 element (Pribnow box in bacteria), -35 element
  • Binding sites for transcription factors and regulatory proteins

Downstream Sequences

  • Located after the transcription start site (positive numbers)
  • Include the transcribed region and termination signals
  • Example: +1 to +... (gene coding region)
  • May contain regulatory elements that affect transcription elongation

5. Transcription Bubble

The transcription bubble is a localized region of DNA unwinding where RNA synthesis occurs.

Structure and Function

  • Approximately 14-17 base pairs of DNA are unwound
  • RNA polymerase moves along the template strand
  • RNA-DNA hybrid forms within the bubble (8-9 bp)
  • DNA rewinds behind the moving polymerase
  • Maintains a consistent size during elongation
  • Creates positive supercoiling ahead and negative supercoiling behind

Transcription Bubble Dynamics

The bubble moves with RNA polymerase during elongation. The size remains relatively constant as DNA unwinds ahead and rewinds behind the polymerase.

Key components of the transcription bubble:

  • Unwound DNA region
  • RNA-DNA hybrid
  • Growing RNA transcript
  • RNA polymerase enzyme
  • Template and non-template DNA strands

Closed Complex

• RNA polymerase binds to promoter but DNA remains double-stranded

• Initial recognition step before bubble formation

• DNA is not yet unwound

• Reversible binding state

Open Complex (Transcription Bubble)

• DNA strands separate forming the transcription bubble

• Approximately 14-17 base pairs are unwound

• Template strand accessible for RNA synthesis

• Irreversible commitment to transcription initiation

Elongation Complex

• RNA polymerase moves along DNA template

• Transcription bubble moves with polymerase

• RNA-DNA hybrid maintained within bubble

• DNA rewinds behind the polymerase

• RNA transcript elongates progressively

6. Components on DNA Required for Transcription: Transcription Unit

A transcription unit is a DNA sequence that is transcribed into a single RNA molecule.

Essential Components

  • Promoter: RNA polymerase binding site
  • Transcription Start Site: Where transcription begins (+1)
  • Coding Sequence: DNA segment that is transcribed
  • Terminator: Sequence that signals transcription end
Transcription Unit Structure:
[Promoter] - [Transcription Start Site] - [RNA Coding Region] - [Terminator]

Transcription Machinery

  • RNA Polymerase: Enzyme that catalyzes RNA synthesis
  • Transcription Factors: Proteins that regulate transcription
  • NTPs: Ribonucleotide triphosphates (ATP, GTP, CTP, UTP)
  • Mg²⁺ ions: Cofactors required for catalytic activity
  • DNA Template: Provides the sequence information

Promoter

• DNA sequence where RNA polymerase binds to initiate transcription

• Contains specific elements recognized by transcription machinery

• Determines transcription start site and efficiency

• Can be regulated by transcription factors and epigenetic modifications

RNA Polymerase

• Enzyme that synthesizes RNA from DNA template

• In bacteria: single RNA polymerase for all transcription

• In eukaryotes: RNA polymerase I, II, and III with different functions

• RNA polymerase II transcribes mRNA

• Requires Mg²⁺ ions as cofactors

Transcription Factors

• Proteins that regulate transcription by binding to DNA

• General transcription factors required for all transcription

• Specific transcription factors regulate particular genes

• Can be activators or repressors

• May respond to cellular signals or environmental cues

Terminator

• DNA sequence that signals the end of transcription

• In bacteria: often a GC-rich region followed by poly-U

• In eukaryotes: cleavage and polyadenylation signals

• Causes RNA polymerase to dissociate from DNA

• Ensures proper transcript length and processing
