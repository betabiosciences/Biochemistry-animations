Tertiary Structure of Proteins

An interactive guide to the complex world of protein folding.

1. Definition of Tertiary Structure

The tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional shape of a single polypeptide chain, stabilized by a variety of interactions between the amino acid side chains. Unlike the primary structure (amino acid sequence) and secondary structure (alpha-helices and beta-sheets), the tertiary structure is the complete, folded functional form of a protein. This intricate shape is essential for a protein's biological activity, as it creates the specific pockets and surfaces needed to bind to other molecules.

2. Stabilizing Interactions

The stability of a protein's tertiary structure is maintained by four primary types of non-covalent interactions and one covalent bond. These forces act between the R-groups (side chains) of the amino acids.

  • Hydrophobic Interactions: Nonpolar amino acids (e.g., Leucine, Valine) tend to cluster together in the protein's core, away from the surrounding aqueous environment. This is the main driving force of protein folding.
  • Hydrogen Bonds: Form between polar side chains, or between polar side chains and the peptide backbone.
  • Ionic Bonds (Salt Bridges): Electrostatic attractions between a positively charged side chain (e.g., Lysine, Arginine) and a negatively charged side chain (e.g., Aspartate, Glutamate).
  • Disulfide Bridges: A strong covalent bond between the sulfur atoms of two cysteine residues. This is a crucial stabilizing force, especially for extracellular proteins.

3. Levinthal's Paradox

Levinthal’s paradox is a thought experiment in protein folding proposed by Cyrus Levinthal in 1969 to illustrate how remarkable it is that proteins fold so quickly into their functional 3D structures despite the astronomical number of possible conformations.

The setup of the paradox

A protein is a linear chain of amino acids. Each amino acid can theoretically adopt multiple rotational conformations (torsion angles $\phi$ and $\psi$) in the polypeptide backbone. For example, if we conservatively assume:

  • 100 amino acids in the chain
  • 3 possible conformations for each residue’s backbone

Then total possible conformations = $3^{100} \approx 5 \times 10^{47}$ possibilities.

The problem

If a protein tried all these conformations randomly:

  • Assume each trial takes $10^{-13}$ seconds (time scale of molecular vibration).
  • Total time to try all possibilities:

    $$ \frac{5 \times 10^{47} \text{ conformations}}{10^{-13} \text{ per second}} = 5 \times 10^{34} \text{ seconds} $$

This is much longer than the age of the universe ($\approx 4.3 \times 10^{17}$ seconds). Yet in reality, proteins fold in microseconds to seconds.

4. Anfinsen's Experiment and its Analysis

Christian Anfinsen's Nobel Prize-winning experiment with ribonuclease A demonstrated a fundamental principle of protein folding. His work showed that the primary structure of a protein contains all the information needed for it to spontaneously fold into its native, three-dimensional structure.

The Three Experimental Conditions

  • Condition 1: Native State
    The initial ribonuclease A protein was fully functional and had the correct tertiary structure with four specific disulfide bridges. The observation was that the enzyme was catalytically active.
  • Condition 2: Denaturation and Reduction
    Anfinsen treated the native protein with urea (to break non-covalent bonds) and 2-mercaptoethanol (to reduce the four disulfide bridges, breaking the covalent S-S bonds). The observation was that the protein became unfolded and inactive.
  • Condition 3: Renaturation
    He then removed the denaturing agents (urea and 2-mercaptoethanol). The observation was that the unfolded protein spontaneously refolded into its native, active conformation, with the correct disulfide bridges reforming. This proved that the amino acid sequence alone dictates the final folded structure.

Hypothetical Disulfide Bond Calculation

If the disulfide bridges in the denatured protein were to form randomly, what is the probability of getting the correct native structure? Ribonuclease A has 8 cysteine residues. The number of ways to form 4 pairs of disulfide bonds from 8 cysteines is given by the formula:

$$ \text{Number of combinations} = \frac{(2n)!}{n! \cdot 2^n} $$

For ribonuclease A, $2n = 8$ (cysteine residues) so $n=4$ (disulfide bonds).

$$ \frac{8!}{4! \cdot 2^4} = \frac{40320}{24 \cdot 16} = \frac{40320}{384} = 105 $$

This shows there are 105 possible ways to form disulfide bridges from 8 cysteine residues, but Anfinsen's experiment showed the protein only formed one correct pattern, reinforcing the idea that the protein's primary sequence guides the folding process.

5. Theories of Protein Folding

There are several models that explain how proteins can fold so quickly and efficiently. These theories help us understand the energetic and physical principles that govern this complex process.

  • Thermodynamic Theory (The Folding Funnel): This theory explains how a protein navigates its vast conformational space to find the native structure. The folding process is visualized as a funnel where the polypeptide chain starts at the wide mouth (high energy, many conformations) and moves down towards the narrow bottom (low energy, native conformation). Each step reduces the number of possible states and increases stability.
  • The "Fly-Casting" Model: This model, particularly relevant for larger proteins, suggests that folding begins with the formation of a loose, partially folded structure. The rest of the chain, like a fly fisherman's line, then "casts" into this pre-formed structure, rapidly finding its correct position. This avoids the need to search the entire conformational space.

6. Protein Denaturation

Denaturation is the process where a protein loses its tertiary (and secondary) structure due to external stresses or compounds, such as high temperature, strong acids/bases, organic solvents, or certain chemicals like urea. This disruption of the non-covalent interactions and disulfide bridges leads to the loss of its biological function. While some proteins can be renatured (as seen in Anfinsen's experiment), this process is often irreversible.

Denaturation as a Cooperative Process

Protein denaturation is often a highly cooperative process. This means that once a part of the protein begins to unfold, it destabilizes other parts of the structure, causing the entire protein to unfold very quickly over a narrow temperature range. This can be visualized with a denaturation curve, where the fraction of unfolded protein changes sharply with temperature.
