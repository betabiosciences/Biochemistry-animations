Overall Features of Secretory Pathway

The secretory pathwayA series of cellular compartments and processes responsible for synthesizing, modifying, and transporting proteins and lipids is a highly organized intracellular logistics system responsible for the synthesis, processing, and sorting of proteins destined for the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, plasma membrane, or the extracellular space.

Key Stages:

  1. Synthesis and translocation: Proteins are synthesized on ribosomes and translocated into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This process can occur co-translationally (during synthesis) or post-translationally (after synthesis).
  2. ER processing and quality control: Proteins fold with the assistance of chaperones and undergo initial modifications like N-linked glycosylation. Misfolded proteins are retained and targeted for degradation via ER-associated degradation (ERAD).
  3. Vesicular transport: Properly folded cargo moves from ER to Golgi apparatus via COPII-coated vesicles. This transport occurs through the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC).
  4. Golgi processing: Further modifications and sorting occur as proteins traverse Golgi cisternae. The Golgi acts as a processing and distribution center.
  5. Targeting and fusion: Transport vesicles deliver cargo to specific destinations like plasma membrane, lysosomes, or secretory granules. This process involves specific targeting signals and SNARE proteins.

The pathway operates through a series of membrane-bound compartments and vesicular carriers, maintaining strict quality control mechanisms to ensure only properly folded proteins proceed. This system is conserved across eukaryotic organisms and is essential for cellular homeostasis, communication, and response to environmental cues.

Cellular Significance

The secretory pathway is crucial for numerous cellular functions including:

  • Production of extracellular matrix components
  • Secretion of hormones, enzymes, and antibodies
  • Delivery of membrane proteins and lipids
  • Formation of lysosomal enzymes
  • Cell surface receptor expression

Anterograde Transport

Anterograde transport moves cargo from the ER through the Golgi apparatus toward final destinations. This forward movement is primarily mediated by COPII-coated vesicles for the initial ER-to-Golgi step.

COPII Vesicles

COPII vesicles are small transport carriers (60-80 nm in diameter) that form at specialized ER exit sites. Their assembly requires the small GTPase Sar1 and the coat proteins Sec23/Sec24 and Sec13/Sec31.

Factors Determining Cargo Recruitment to COPII Vesicles:

Signals:

  • ER export signals: Specific amino acid motifs that interact with COPII components. These include:
    • Di-acidic motifs (e.g., Asp-X-Glu) recognized by Sec24
    • Di-hydrophobic motifs: (e.g., FF, YY, or LL) at the C-terminus.
  • Transmembrane domain features: This is a lipid-based sorting mechanism. The ER membrane is relatively thin and loose. The Golgi membrane is thicker and rich in sphingolipids/cholesterol. Proteins with longer TMDs are energetically excluded from the thin ER membrane and preferentially partition into forming COPII vesicles, which have a lipid composition intermediate between ER and Golgi.

Size of Transmembrane Domain:

The length of transmembrane domains (TMDs) significantly impacts ER export efficiency through several mechanisms:

  • Proteins with TMDs shorter than 17 amino acids are often retained in the ER due to inefficient packing into the lipid bilayer.
  • Optimal TMD length (typically 17-21 residues) facilitates efficient packaging into COPII vesicles by providing sufficient hydrophobic interactions.

Vesicle Formation and Cargo Selection

The process of COPII vesicle formation involves sequential recruitment of coat proteins:

  1. Activation of Sar1 by Sec12, leading to membrane association
  2. Recruitment of Sec23/Sec24 complex, which directly binds cargo
  3. Addition of Sec13/Sec31 outer coat, driving membrane curvature
  4. Membrane scission to release the completed vesicle

These selective mechanisms ensure proper cargo sorting while preventing premature export of immature proteins or ER residents.

Structure and Organization of the Golgi Apparatus

The Golgi apparatus is a central organelle in the secretory pathway, organized as a series of flattened membrane-bound cisternae stacked upon each other. It serves as the major processing and sorting station for proteins and lipids.

Structural Components:

  • Cis-Golgi network (CGN): The entry face receiving vesicles from the ER and ERGIC. It functions in cargo reception and early sorting decisions. It determines if proteins proceed forward or are returned to the ER.
  • Cis-Golgi: The first stable compartment of the Golgi stack proper.
    • Located immediately after the CGN, represents the first processing station
    • Contains enzymes for early carbohydrate modifications including mannosidase I
    • Initiates processing of N-linked glycans by trimming mannose residues
    • Maintains pH around 6.5-6.7, optimal for early processing enzymes
  • Medial Golgi: Intermediate cisternae where most modifications occur. These compartments contain the majority of Golgi processing enzymes.
  • Trans-Golgi: The final processing compartment before the TGN.
    • Site of terminal glycosylation reactions including sialylation and galactosylation
    • Contains sulfotransferases for tyrosine and glycosaminoglycan sulfation
    • Performs proteolytic processing of proproteins into active forms
    • More acidic environment (pH ~6.2-6.4) that facilitates late-stage modifications
    • Coordinates with TGN for final sorting decisions
  • Trans-Golgi network (TGN): The exit face where final processing, sorting, and packaging into transport vesicles occurs. It serves as a major branch point for different destinations.It is a sorting hub where cargo is packaged into clathrin-coated vesicles (for lysosomes), secretory granules (regulated secretion), or constitutive vesicles.
  • Golgi matrix: The Golgi matrix refers to the dense, proteinaceous scaffold that surrounds and organizes the Golgi apparatus.Structural proteins (golgins, GRASPs) that maintain Golgi architecture and facilitate vesicle tethering. The matrix provides structural stability while allowing dynamic reorganization.

Functional Organization:

The Golgi exhibits both structural and functional polarity, with compartmentalization of processing enzymes:

  • Compartmentalization of enzymes: Different Golgi subcompartments contain distinct sets of modification enzymes, creating an assembly-line system for sequential processing.
  • Cisternal progression: Cisternae mature from cis to trans positions over time, carrying cargo through different processing environments.
  • Vesicular transport: COPI vesicles mediate intra-Golgi transport and retrieval of escaped ER residents and Golgi enzymes.
  • pH gradient: The Golgi maintains a pH gradient from approximately 6.7 in the cis-Golgi to 6.0 in the TGN, which optimizes enzyme activities and facilitates sorting.

Retrograde Transport from Golgi to ER

Retrograde transport moves proteins and lipids from the Golgi back to the ER, maintaining organelle identity and retrieving escaped ER residents. This process is essential for the dynamic equilibrium of the secretory pathway.

COPI Vesicles

COPI vesicles (70-90 nm) mediate retrograde transport within the Golgi and from the Golgi to ER. Their formation requires the small GTPase Arf1 and the coatomer complex, a heptameric protein complex.

Mechanisms:

  • COPI-coated vesicles: Mediate retrograde transport from Golgi to ER and within the Golgi stack. COPI vesicles are characterized by their coatomer coat and are slightly larger than COPII vesicles.
  • KDEL receptors: Recognize C-terminal KDEL sequences on soluble ER proteins and retrieve them from the Golgi. The binding is pH-dependent, with stronger binding in the slightly acidic Golgi environment.
  • Transmembrane protein retrieval: Cytoplasmic di-lysine (KKXX) or dibasic motifs interact with COPI components for retrieval. These motifs are recognized by specific subunits of the coatomer complex.

Significance:

  • Maintenance of organelle identity: Retrieves escaped ER-resident proteins, preventing their dilution throughout the secretory pathway.
  • Quality control: Returns misfolded proteins for ER-associated degradation (ERAD). Some quality control mechanisms operate through continuous retrieval cycles.
  • Recycling of machinery: Retrieves vesicle components like SNAREs, cargo receptors, and modification enzymes, making the secretory pathway economically efficient.
  • Lipid homeostasis: Balances lipid composition between organelles and retrieves lipid-processing enzymes to their proper locations.
  • Golgi enzyme distribution: Maintains the compartmentalization of Golgi processing enzymes through continuous retrieval during cisternal maturation.

Retrograde Transport in Cisternal Maturation

The cisternal maturation model proposes that Golgi cisternae themselves progress from cis to trans positions while resident enzymes move in the retrograde direction. This model provides an elegant explanation for how large cargo can traverse the Golgi without fragmentation.

Cisternal Maturation

COPI vesicles do not carry cargo forward; instead, they strip enzymes from a "maturing" cisterna and move them backward to a younger cisterna behind it.

Key Principles:

  • Cisternal progression: New cisternae form at the cis face by fusion of ER-derived vesicles and progressively mature into trans cisternae over 10-20 minutes.
  • Enzyme recycling: Golgi-resident enzymes are continuously retrieved by COPI vesicles and moved backward to younger cisternae. This maintains the enzyme gradient across the stack.
  • Cargo progression: Secretory cargo remains within maturing cisternae while enzymes are recycled. This allows cargo to be sequentially processed by different enzyme complements.

Detailed Process:

  1. Vesicles from the ER and ERGIC fuse to form a new cis-cisterna
  2. This cisterna begins to acquire medial Golgi enzymes through COPI-mediated retrograde transport from more mature cisternae
  3. As the cisterna moves toward the trans face, it loses cis-enzymes and gains trans-enzymes through continued retrograde transport
  4. At the trans face, the cisterna loses its identity as cargo is packaged into transport carriers and the remaining membrane fragments or recycles
  5. Throughout this process, COPI vesicles continuously redistribute enzymes to maintain the compartment-specific enzyme distribution

Modifications in the Cargo Protein from Golgi

The Golgi apparatus performs crucial post-translational modifications that determine protein function, stability, and destination. These modifications occur in a compartment-specific manner as cargo progresses through the Golgi stack.

Compartmentalization of Modifications

Different Golgi compartments contain specific enzyme complements: cis-Golgi - early processing; medial Golgi - intermediate processing; trans-Golgi - late processing; TGN - sorting and packaging.

Major Modification Types:

Glycosylation:

  • N-linked glycosylation processing: High-mannose oligosaccharides added in the ER are processed to complex/hybrid forms through sequential removal and addition of sugars by:
    • Mannosidases I and II that trim mannose residues
    • N-acetylglucosaminyltransferases that add GlcNAc
    • Galactosyltransferases that add galactose
    • Sialyltransferases that add sialic acid
  • O-linked glycosylation: Addition of sugars to serine/threonine residues, initiated in Golgi by addition of N-acetylgalactosamine followed by elongation.
  • Glycosaminoglycan (GAG) addition: On proteoglycans, involving the formation of long, unbranched polysaccharide chains.
  • Glycolipid formation: Addition of carbohydrate groups to lipid molecules, particularly important for membrane composition and signaling.

Proteolytic Processing:

  • Cleavage of proproteins to active forms (e.g., prohormones like proinsulin to insulin, proenzymes like trypsinogen to trypsin).
  • Trimming of signal peptides or propeptides by specific proteases.
  • Activation of growth factors and neuropeptides through limited proteolysis.
  • Processing of viral envelope proteins in infected cells.

Other Modifications:

  • Sulfation: Addition of sulfate groups to tyrosine residues (tyrosine sulfation) or carbohydrates (glycosaminoglycan sulfation) by sulfotransferases using PAPS as sulfate donor.PAPS (3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate) is the universal sulfate donor in biological sulfate transfer reactions.
  • Phosphorylation: On carbohydrate moieties (mannose-6-phosphate targeting signal for lysosomes) or protein residues.
  • Lipidation: Addition of lipid groups like GPI anchors that tether proteins to the membrane exterior.

The sequential nature of these modifications in different Golgi compartments ensures proper processing and creates checkpoints for quality control before proteins reach their final destinations.
