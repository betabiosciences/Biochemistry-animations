Electron Transport Chain

Reduction Potential & Its Significance

The reduction potential (E°) is a measure of the tendency of a chemical species to acquire electrons and be reduced. In the electron transport chain, this concept is fundamental to understanding how electrons flow from one carrier to another.

Key Concepts:

Standard Reduction Potential: Measured under standard conditions (25°C, 1 atm, 1M concentrations) and expressed in volts (V). More positive values indicate a greater tendency to be reduced.

E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode

Significance in ETC:

Electrons flow spontaneously from carriers with lower (more negative) reduction potentials to those with higher (more positive) reduction potentials. This creates a "waterfall" of electron flow that releases energy.

ETC Reduction Potential Gradient:

• NADH/NAD+ = -0.32 V

• Complex I = -0.30 V

• CoQ/CoQH₂ = +0.04 V

• Complex III = +0.22 V

• Cytochrome c = +0.25 V

• Complex IV = +0.35 V

• O₂/H₂O = +0.82 V

This gradient ensures that electron flow is thermodynamically favorable and provides the driving force for ATP synthesis.

Bound vs Mobile Electron Carriers

Bound Electron Carriers

These carriers are permanently attached to protein complexes and cannot move freely within the membrane.

Examples of Bound Carriers:

FMN (Flavin Mononucleotide): Found in Complex I, accepts 2 electrons and 2 protons

Iron-Sulfur Clusters: Present in Complexes I, II, and III, contain iron atoms coordinated with sulfur

Heme Groups: Found in Complexes III and IV, contain iron atoms in porphyrin rings

Copper Centers: Located in Complex IV, participate in oxygen reduction

Mobile Electron Carriers

These carriers can move freely within the inner mitochondrial membrane, shuttling electrons between complexes.

Key Mobile Carriers:

Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone/Ubiquinol):

• Lipid-soluble molecule that shuttles between Complexes I/II and III

• Exists in oxidized (ubiquinone) and reduced (ubiquinol) forms

• Can accept 1 or 2 electrons, forming semiquinone intermediate

Cytochrome c:

• Small, water-soluble protein in the intermembrane space

• Shuttles electrons between Complexes III and IV

• Contains a heme group that alternates between Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺

The combination of bound and mobile carriers creates an efficient electron transfer system that spans the entire inner mitochondrial membrane.

Complex I - NADH Dehydrogenase

Complex I is the largest protein complex in the ETC, containing 45 subunits and serving as the entry point for electrons from NADH.

Electron Flow Route:

NADH → FMN → Fe-S clusters → CoQ → CoQH₂

Redox Centers in Complex I:

1. FMN (Flavin Mononucleotide): Initial electron acceptor from NADH

2. Iron-Sulfur Clusters (7 clusters):

• N1a, N1b: [2Fe-2S] clusters

• N2, N3, N4, N5, N6a, N6b: [4Fe-4S] clusters

3. Ubiquinone Binding Site: Final electron acceptor

Proton Pumping Mechanism:

Conformational Coupling Model:

• Electron transfer through Fe-S clusters induces conformational changes

• These changes alter the pKa of ionizable groups

• Protons are picked up from the matrix and released to the intermembrane space

• The process is driven by the energy released from electron transfer

Stoichiometry: For each NADH oxidized, Complex I pumps 4 protons across the membrane while transferring 2 electrons to CoQ.

NADH + H⁺ + CoQ + 4H⁺(matrix) → NAD⁺ + CoQH₂ + 4H⁺(intermembrane space)

Complex II - Succinate Dehydrogenase

Complex II is unique as it serves dual roles: it's both part of the citric acid cycle and the ETC. It does not pump protons.

Electron Flow Route:

FADH₂ → FAD → Fe-S clusters → Heme b → CoQ → CoQH₂

Redox Centers in Complex II:

1. FAD (Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide): Covalently bound to succinate dehydrogenase

2. Iron-Sulfur Clusters (3 clusters):

• [2Fe-2S] cluster

• Two [4Fe-4S] clusters

3. Heme b: Low-potential heme group

4. Ubiquinone Binding Sites: Two distinct binding sites (QP and QD)

No Proton Pumping:

Why Complex II Doesn't Pump Protons:

• Lower energy yield from FADH₂ oxidation compared to NADH

• Insufficient free energy change to drive proton translocation

• Functions primarily as an entry point for electrons from succinate oxidation

Function: Complex II channels electrons from FADH₂ directly into the CoQ pool, bypassing the proton-pumping step of Complex I.

FADH₂ + CoQ → FAD + CoQH₂

Complex III - Cytochrome bc₁ Complex

Complex III facilitates electron transfer from CoQH₂ to cytochrome c through the famous Q-cycle mechanism.

Electron Flow Route:

CoQH₂ → Rieske Fe-S → Cytochrome c₁ → Cytochrome c

Redox Centers in Complex III:

1. Cytochrome b: Contains two heme groups (bL and bH)

2. Rieske Iron-Sulfur Protein: [2Fe-2S] cluster with histidine ligands

3. Cytochrome c₁: Contains heme c group

4. Two CoQ Binding Sites:

• Qo site (near intermembrane space)

• Qi site (near matrix)

Q-Cycle Mechanism (Detailed)

First Half of Q-Cycle:

1. CoQH₂ binds to Qo site and releases 2H⁺ to intermembrane space

2. First electron: CoQH₂ → Rieske Fe-S → cyt c₁ → cyt c

3. Second electron: CoQ•⁻ → cyt bL → cyt bH → CoQ at Qi site

4. CoQ at Qi site becomes CoQ•⁻ (semiquinone)

Second Half of Q-Cycle:

5. Another CoQH₂ repeats the process at Qo site

6. The second electron reduces CoQ•⁻ at Qi site to CoQH₂

7. 2H⁺ from matrix are consumed to form CoQH₂ at Qi site

Net Result:

CoQH₂ + 2cyt c(Fe³⁺) + 2H⁺(matrix) → CoQ + 2cyt c(Fe²⁺) + 4H⁺(intermembrane space)

Proton Pumping: Complex III pumps 4 protons for every 2 electrons transferred, with 2H⁺ coming from CoQH₂ oxidation and 2H⁺ consumed from the matrix.

Complex IV - Cytochrome c Oxidase

Complex IV is the terminal oxidase that catalyzes the reduction of oxygen to water, completing the electron transport chain.

Electron Flow Route:

Cytochrome c → CuA → Heme a → Heme a₃-CuB → O₂ → H₂O

Redox Centers in Complex IV:

1. CuA Center: Binuclear copper center, initial electron acceptor

2. Heme a: Low-spin heme group

3. Binuclear Center:

• Heme a₃: High-spin heme with iron

• CuB: Copper center coupled to heme a₃

• This is where O₂ binding and reduction occurs

Proton Pumping Mechanism:

Electrostatic Coupling Model:

• Electron transfer creates charge separation

• Conformational changes open and close proton channels

• Two types of protons:

  - Chemical protons: Used for water formation

  - Pumped protons: Translocated across membrane

Oxygen Reduction:

O₂ + 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2H₂O

Stoichiometry: Complex IV pumps 2 protons for every 2 electrons transferred (4 electrons total for complete O₂ reduction).

4cyt c(Fe²⁺) + O₂ + 8H⁺(matrix) → 4cyt c(Fe³⁺) + 2H₂O + 4H⁺(intermembrane space)

Proton Pumping Summary & Electrochemical Gradient

Proton Pumping Stoichiometry:

NADH Oxidation (per NADH):

• Complex I: 4 H⁺

• Complex III: 4 H⁺

• Complex IV: 2 H⁺

Total: 10 H⁺ pumped per NADH

FADH₂ Oxidation (per FADH₂):

• Complex II: 0 H⁺ (no pumping)

• Complex III: 4 H⁺

• Complex IV: 2 H⁺

Total: 6 H⁺ pumped per FADH₂

The Electrochemical Gradient (Proton-Motive Force):

The pumping of protons creates an electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, consisting of two components:

1. Chemical Gradient (ΔpH):

• Higher H⁺ concentration in intermembrane space

• Lower H⁺ concentration in matrix

• Typical ΔpH ≈ 0.5-1.0 units

• Matrix pH ≈ 7.8, intermembrane space pH ≈ 7.0

2. Electrical Gradient (Δψ):

• Positive charge accumulation in intermembrane space

• Negative charge in matrix (due to H⁺ removal)

• Typical membrane potential ≈ -180 to -200 mV

• Matrix is negative relative to intermembrane space

Biological Significance:

This electrochemical gradient serves as the driving force for ATP synthesis through ATP synthase (Complex V). The stored energy is equivalent to approximately 50-60 kJ/mol, providing ample energy for ATP formation from ADP and Pi.

ADP + Pi + nH⁺(intermembrane space) → ATP + H₂O + nH⁺(matrix)

Where n ≈ 3-4 protons per ATP

This completes our journey through the electron transport chain - from the initial reduction potentials to the final electrochemical gradient that powers life!
