Mismatch DNA Repair

1. Features & Significance

Mismatch Repair (MMR) is a crucial post-replicative proofreading system that scans the genome for errors that have escaped the 3'→5' exonuclease activity of DNA polymerase.

Key Significance

  • Genomic Fidelity: MMR increases the fidelity of DNA replication by 100- to 1000-fold, acting as a critical barrier against spontaneous mutation.
  • Error Correction: It primarily corrects base-base mismatches (e.g., G-T, A-C) and small insertion/deletion loops (IDLs) that arise from polymerase "slippage," especially in repetitive DNA regions (microsatellites).
  • Genome Stability: By preventing mutation accumulation, MMR is essential for maintaining genomic integrity and preventing cancer.

Core Features

  • Post-Replicative: It operates *after* the replication fork has passed.
  • Strand Discrimination: The most critical feature. The system must "know" which of the two mismatched bases is the correct one (on the template strand) and which is the error (on the newly synthesized strand). The mechanisms for this differ between prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
  • Conserved Components: The core machinery involves two key protein families:
    • MutS homologs: Recognize the mismatch.
    • MutL homologs: Act as matchmakers, linking MutS to downstream excision and resynthesis proteins.

2. Prokaryotic Mechanism (E. coli)

The E. coli model is the classic example, relying on DNA methylation for strand discrimination.

Components

  • Dam Methylase: Methylates the adenine in GATC sequences.
  • MutS: A homodimer that scans DNA and binds to the mismatch.
  • MutL: A homodimer that binds MutS and activates MutH.
  • MutH: A strand-specific endonuclease.
  • UvrD (Helicase II) & Exonucleases: To unwind and degrade the error-containing strand.
  • DNA Pol III & Ligase: To resynthesize and seal.

Step-by-Step Process

  1. Recognition: MutS scans the DNA, recognizes the distortion from the mismatch, and binds to it.
  2. Assembly: MutL is recruited, forming a MutS-MutL complex. This complex translocates along the DNA.
  3. Strand Discrimination: The DNA is transiently "hemimethylated" after replication. The template strand is methylated at GATC sites, but the new strand is not yet. The MutS-MutL complex finds the *nearest* hemimethylated GATC site.
  4. Incision: MutL activates the MutH endonuclease, which nicks the *unmethylated* (new) strand at the GATC site. This nick can be 5' or 3' to the mismatch.
  5. Excision: UvrD (Helicase II) unwinds the nicked strand. Based on the nick's location, an exonuclease (e.g., ExoVII or RecJ for a 5' nick; ExoI for a 3' nick) degrades the new strand from the nick *past* the mismatch.
  6. Resynthesis: DNA Pol III fills the large gap using the intact template strand.
  7. Ligation: DNA ligase seals the final nick.

3. Eukaryotic Mechanism

Eukaryotic MMR is more complex and does not use methylation for strand discrimination. It is tightly coupled to the replication fork.

Components (Human)

  • MutS Homologs (Heterodimers):
    • MutSα (MSH2-MSH6): Recognizes base-base mismatches and small (1-2 bp) IDLs.
    • MutSβ (MSH2-MSH3): Recognizes larger IDLs (up to ~16 bp).
  • MutL Homologs (Heterodimers):
    • MutLα (MLH1-PMS2): The primary complex. Crucially, PMS2 has a cryptic endonuclease activity.
    • MutLβ (MLH1-PMS1) & MutLγ (MLH1-MLH3): Other complexes with more specialized roles.
  • PCNA, RPA, EXO1, Pol δ, Ligase I

Step-by-Step Process

  1. Recognition: MutSα (or MutSβ) binds the mismatch.
  2. Assembly: MutLα is recruited. The complex is loaded onto DNA in an ATP-dependent manner, likely sliding.
  3. Strand Discrimination: This is the key difference. It is linked to replication. The newly synthesized strand is identified by its ends, specifically the nicks present in Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand or transient nicks on the leading strand. PCNA (Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen), the sliding clamp, is enriched on the new strand and helps orient the MMR machinery.
  4. Incision: The endonuclease activity of MutLα (PMS2) is activated upon interaction with the MutS-mismatch complex and PCNA. It introduces a nick in the new (error-containing) strand.
  5. Excision: EXO1 (Exonuclease 1) is recruited and, in a 5'→3' direction, degrades the new strand from the nick past the mismatch. RPA (Replication Protein A) coats the exposed single strand.
  6. Resynthesis: DNA Polymerase δ (delta), loaded by PCNA, fills the gap.
  7. Ligation: DNA Ligase I seals the nick.

4. Defects in Mismatch Repair

Failure of the MMR system has severe consequences, leading to a "mutator phenotype" and a high predisposition to cancer.

Microsatellite Instability (MSI)

The hallmark of a defective MMR system. Microsatellites are short, repetitive DNA sequences (e.g., -CACACA-). During replication, DNA polymerase is prone to "slipping" on these repeats, creating small IDLs.

  • In a healthy cell, MMR efficiently recognizes and repairs these slippage-induced IDLs.
  • In an MMR-deficient cell, these errors are not corrected. This results in microsatellites expanding or contracting in length, a state known as Microsatellite Instability (MSI).

Lynch Syndrome (HNPCC)

  • Definition: The most common hereditary cancer syndrome. It is an autosomal dominant condition caused by a germline mutation in one copy of an MMR gene (most commonly MLH1 or MSH2, also MSH6 or PMS2).
  • Mechanism: Individuals are born with one mutated allele. A "second hit" (somatic mutation or epigenetic silencing) that inactivates the remaining healthy allele in a cell leads to complete MMR deficiency.
  • Result: That cell rapidly accumulates mutations (MSI) and progresses to cancer, particularly colorectal, endometrial, ovarian, and stomach cancers.
