Eukaryotic Replication Initiation

Overview: Selection, Licensing, and Firing

The initiation of DNA replication in eukaryotes is a tightly regulated, multi-step process ensuring the genome is duplicated exactly once per cell cycle. It is broadly divided into three main phases, each occurring at a distinct point in the cell cycle:

  • Replicator Selection (G1 Phase): Specific sites on the chromosome, known as origins of replication, are identified and marked.
  • Origin Licensing (G1 Phase): The core replicative machinery, the MCM helicase, is loaded onto these selected origins to form the pre-replicative complex (pre-RC). This "licenses" the origin, making it competent for firing.
  • Origin Firing (S Phase): Upon entry into S phase, these licensed origins are activated by specific kinases. The helicases unwind the DNA, and DNA synthesis begins.

Temporal Separation of Licensing and Firing

The most critical control mechanism is the temporal separation of licensing (G1) and firing (S phase). This strict separation prevents disastrous re-replication of DNA segments. This control is primarily exerted by Cyclin-Dependent Kinases (CDKs).

  • Low CDK Activity (G1 Phase): In G1, CDK activity is kept low (primarily by the APC/C-Cdh1 complex). This permits origin licensing. The factors required to load the MCM helicase (like Cdc6 and Cdt1) are stable and active.
  • High CDK Activity (S, G2, M Phases): As the cell transitions to S phase, CDK activity rises sharply. This high CDK activity has two opposing functions:
    1. It triggers origin firing by phosphorylating key initiation factors.
    2. It inhibits re-licensing by phosphorylating and promoting the degradation or inhibition of the G1 licensing factors (ORC, Cdc6, Cdt1).

Thus, licensing can only happen when firing is inhibited (G1), and firing can only happen when licensing is inhibited (S phase).

The Replicator and The Initiator

These two components form the foundation of origin selection, based on the classic "replicon" model.

  • The Replicator: This is the cis-acting DNA sequence that defines an origin of replication.
    • In budding yeast, this is a well-defined 100-150 bp sequence called the ARS (Autonomously Replicating Sequence).
    • In metazoans (like humans), replicators are much less defined by a specific sequence. Their location is often influenced by chromatin structure, epigenetic marks (like histone modifications), and DNA topology.
  • The Initiator: This is the trans-acting protein complex that binds specifically to the replicator to begin the process.
    • In all eukaryotes, the initiator is the Origin Recognition Complex (ORC).
    • ORC is a six-subunit (Orc1-6) complex that binds ATP. It acts as the central "landing pad" or "scaffold" upon which the entire pre-replicative complex is built.

DNA Helicase and Helicase Loaders

The central engine of replication is the DNA helicase, which must be loaded onto the origin long before it is activated.

  • The Replicative Helicase: This is the MCM (Minichromosome Maintenance) complex, composed of six related subunits (MCM2-7). This hexameric ring is the core of the active helicase.
  • Helicase Loaders: The MCM complex cannot load onto DNA by itself. This process is mediated by two key factors in G1:
    • Cdc6: An ATPase that is recruited by ORC. It is essential for opening the MCM ring.
    • Cdt1: A protein that chaperones the MCM2-7 hexamer and delivers it to the ORC-Cdc6 complex at the origin.
  • The Result: Together, ORC, Cdc6, and Cdt1 load two MCM2-7 hexamers onto the origin DNA. They are loaded in an inactive, head-to-head orientation, forming the MCM double hexamer (DH). This is the fully licensed pre-RC.

Mechanism of Origin Licensing (G1)

The assembly of the pre-RC is a sequential, ATP-driven process:

  1. The ORC initiator complex is bound to the replicator DNA.
  2. ORC recruits the Cdc6 loader.
  3. The ORC-Cdc6 complex then recruits Cdt1, which is attached to an MCM2-7 hexamer.
  4. Through ATP hydrolysis by ORC and Cdc6, the MCM2-7 ring is opened and loaded around the double-stranded DNA.
  5. This process is repeated to load a second MCM2-7 hexamer, forming the inactive, head-to-head MCM double hexamer. The origin is now licensed.

Mechanism of Origin Firing (S Phase)

Activation of the licensed pre-RC requires two master kinases: S-phase CDK and DDK (Dbf4-dependent kinase).

  1. DDK phosphorylates the MCM subunits, which is thought to "prime" the complex.
  2. S-CDK phosphorylates two key adaptor proteins: Sld2 and Sld3 (in yeast; Treslin and RecQL4 in metazoans).
  3. These phosphorylations create docking sites for other factors, notably Gins and Cdc45.
  4. Gins and Cdc45 are recruited to the MCM double hexamer. Their binding transforms the inactive MCM complex into the active replicative helicase, the CMG complex (Cdc45-MCM-GINS).
  5. The formation of two CMG complexes (one from each MCM hexamer) causes the double hexamer to separate. The two CMG helicases then begin to unwind DNA in opposite directions, creating the replication bubble and recruiting the rest of the replisome.

Primer Synthesis

Once the CMG helicase begins to unwind the DNA, the exposed single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) is immediately coated by RPA (Replication Protein A) to protect it.

  • The Primase-Polymerase α Complex: This four-subunit enzyme is responsible for making primers.
  • Primase Subunit: Synthesizes a short RNA primer (approx. 10-12 nucleotides) directly on the ssDNA template.
  • Polymerase α (alpha) Subunit: Takes over from the primase and extends the RNA primer with a short stretch of DNA (approx. 20-30 nucleotides).

This short RNA-DNA hybrid molecule is the primer that will be recognized and extended by the main replicative polymerases.

Sliding Clamp and Clamp Loader

The short primer made by Pol α-primase is not stable enough for long-stretch synthesis. This triggers a "polymerase switch."

  • The Clamp Loader: This is Replication Factor C (RFC). It is a five-subunit complex that recognizes the 3' end of the primer-template junction created by Pol α.
  • The Sliding Clamp: This is PCNA (Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen). It is a ring-shaped trimer that encircles the DNA.
  • Loading Mechanism: RFC (an ATPase) binds to PCNA and, using ATP, opens the ring and loads it onto the DNA at the primer junction. RFC then detaches.
  • Function: PCNA acts as a "processivity factor." It tethers the main replicative polymerases—DNA Polymerase ε (epsilon) for the leading strand and DNA Polymerase δ (delta) for the lagging strand—firmly to the DNA template. This allows them to synthesize DNA for thousands of base pairs without falling off, displacing the low-processivity Pol α in the process.
