Quaternary Structure of Hemoglobin

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a classic example of a protein with a quaternary structure. It is a globular, tetrameric protein composed of four polypeptide chains, typically two identical α-chains (141 amino acids each) and two identical β-chains (146 amino acids each) in adult hemoglobin (HbA). This is denoted as an α₂β₂ heterotetramer.

Salient Features:

  • Heme Group: Each subunit non-covalently binds a prosthetic group called heme. The heme group consists of a protoporphyrin IX ring with a central ferrous iron atom (Fe²⁺), which is the site of reversible oxygen binding.
  • Subunit Interfaces: The subunits are held together by extensive non-covalent interactions. The α₁β₁ interface is strong, while the α₁β₂ interface is weaker and allows for conformational changes upon oxygenation.
  • T and R States: The quaternary structure exists in two major conformations: the "T" (tense) state (low oxygen affinity) and the "R" (relaxed) state (high oxygen affinity).

Cooperativity and Allosterism

Hemoglobin exhibits allosterism and positive cooperativity. Positive cooperativity means that binding one oxygen molecule increases the affinity of the other heme groups, leading to a sigmoidal oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve.

Mechanism of Cooperativity:

When O₂ binds, it pulls the Fe²⁺ atom into the plane of the porphyrin ring. This movement triggers a conformational change that is transmitted through the subunit to the α₁β₂ interface, destabilizing the T-state and favoring the high-affinity R-state.

Models of Allosterism:

  • MWC (Concerted) Model: Proposes the tetramer exists in only two states (T or R), and ligand binding shifts the equilibrium towards the R state.
  • KNF (Sequential) Model: Suggests ligand binding induces a conformational change in one subunit, which then influences adjacent subunits sequentially.

Oxygen-Hemoglobin Dissociation Curve

The oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve (ODC) plots hemoglobin saturation against the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂). Its sigmoidal shape is a direct consequence of cooperative binding.

  • Steep Region: In peripheral tissues (pO₂ 20-40 mmHg), a small drop in pO₂ releases a large amount of oxygen.
  • Plateau Region: In the lungs (pO₂ ~100 mmHg), hemoglobin is nearly fully saturated, ensuring efficient oxygen uptake.
  • P₅₀ Value: The pO₂ at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated (~26.7 mmHg for HbA). It is a key indicator of oxygen affinity.

The Hill Equation and Plot

The Hill equation mathematically describes cooperative binding.

$$ Y = \frac{(pO_2)^n}{(P_{50})^n + (pO_2)^n} $$

Rearranging for a linear plot (the Hill Plot):

$$ \log\left(\frac{Y}{1-Y}\right) = n \log(pO_2) - n \log(P_{50}) $$

The slope of the Hill Plot is the Hill coefficient (n). For hemoglobin, n ≈ 2.8, indicating positive cooperativity. For myoglobin, n = 1, indicating no cooperativity.

Clinical Correlations & Summary

Bohr Effect

The inverse relationship between pH/CO₂ and O₂ affinity. In active tissues, lower pH and higher CO₂ stabilize the T-state, promoting O₂ release (rightward shift).

Haldane Effect

The reciprocal of the Bohr effect. Deoxygenated hemoglobin has a higher capacity to carry CO₂ and buffer H⁺ ions, facilitating their transport from tissues to the lungs.

Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c)

Formed by the non-enzymatic reaction of glucose with hemoglobin. The percentage of HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over 2-3 months, making it a key marker for diabetes management.
