Features of Nuclear Receptors and Ligands

Nuclear receptors are a family of transcription factors that regulate gene expression in response to specific ligands. They play crucial roles in development, homeostasis, and metabolism.

Key features of nuclear receptors:

  • They are activated by lipophilic ligands such as steroid hormones, thyroid hormones, retinoids, and vitamin D
  • They function as ligand-activated transcription factors
  • They can directly bind to DNA at specific sequences called hormone response elements (HREs)
  • They regulate diverse biological processes including metabolism, reproduction, and development
  • They can act as integrators of physiological signals by responding to hormonal and metabolic cues

Nuclear receptor ligands are typically small, lipophilic molecules that can diffuse across the plasma membrane to bind their cognate receptors inside the cell.

  • A/B Domain (N-terminal domain): Contains activation function 1 (AF-1) that is involved in transcriptional activation. This region is highly variable among different nuclear receptors.
  • C Domain (DNA-binding domain, DBD): Highly conserved region containing two zinc fingers that mediate binding to specific DNA sequences (HREs).
    • P-box: Part of the DBD that determines specificity for the DNA sequence half-site recognition.
    • D-box: Part of the DBD involved in dimerization and spacing recognition between half-sites.
  • D Domain (Hinge region): Flexible region that connects the DBD to the LBD. It can influence DNA binding and contains nuclear localization signals.
  • E Domain (Ligand-binding domain, LBD): Moderately conserved region that binds ligands. It also contains activation function 2 (AF-2) that recruits coactivators upon ligand binding.
  • F Domain (C-terminal domain): A variable region at the C-terminus of some nuclear receptors. Its function is not well characterized and may modulate the activity of the LBD.

Hormone Response Element (HRE)

Hormone Response Elements (HREs) are specific DNA sequences that nuclear receptors bind to regulate transcription of target genes.

Key characteristics of HREs:

  • Typically consist of two half-sites (short sequences of 6 base pairs each)
  • Half-sites can be arranged as inverted repeats (IR), direct repeats (DR), or everted repeats (ER)
  • The spacing between half-sites determines which nuclear receptor will bind
  • The sequence of the half-sites determines receptor specificity

For example, the glucocorticoid response element typically consists of an inverted repeat of the sequence AGAACA with a 3-nucleotide spacer (IR3), while the thyroid hormone response element often consists of a direct repeat of the sequence AGGTCA with a 4-nucleotide spacer (DR4).

Upon ligand binding, nuclear receptors undergo conformational changes that allow them to recruit coactivators or corepressors, leading to activation or repression of target gene transcription.

Types of Nuclear Receptors

Nuclear receptors are classified into four types based on their dimerization and DNA binding properties:

Type I: Steroid Receptors

Examples: Glucocorticoid receptor (GR), Estrogen receptor (ER)

Mechanism: Inactive in cytoplasm, form homodimers upon ligand binding, translocate to nucleus, bind to inverted repeat HREs.

Type II: Non-steroid Receptors

Examples: Thyroid hormone receptor (TR), Retinoic acid receptor (RAR)

Mechanism: Bound to DNA even without ligand, often form heterodimers with RXR, bind to direct repeat HREs.

Type III: Dimerizing Receptors

Examples: Vitamin D receptor (VDR), Retinoid X receptor (RXR)

Mechanism: Form homodimers, bind to direct repeat HREs, spacing between half-sites determines specificity.

Type IV: Orphan Receptors

Examples: SF-1, HNF4α

Mechanism: Ligands unknown or not required, can bind as monomers to extended half-sites, often constitutive activators.

Each receptor type has distinct mechanisms of action, DNA binding preferences, and biological functions, allowing for precise regulation of gene expression in response to diverse signals.
