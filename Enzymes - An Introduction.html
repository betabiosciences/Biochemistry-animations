Enzymes: An Introduction

Exploring the Catalysts of Life

1. Physical Features of Enzymes

Enzymes are fascinating biological molecules with distinct physical properties. Understanding these features is key to grasping how they function.

  • Protein Nature: Almost all enzymes are globular proteins. Their function is intricately linked to their complex three-dimensional structure. A few RNA molecules, called ribozymes, can also act as enzymes.
  • Three-Dimensional Structure: The polypeptide chain of an enzyme folds into a specific 3D shape, creating a unique microenvironment. This structure is crucial and is maintained by various bonds like hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and disulfide bridges.
  • The Active Site: A small, specific region within the enzyme's structure, called the active site, is where the substrate binds and the chemical reaction occurs. The active site has a precise shape and chemical properties that complement its specific substrate(s).
  • Colloidal Nature: Being large molecules, enzymes form colloidal solutions in water. This property provides a large surface area for catalytic activity.
  • Sensitivity: Enzyme structures are sensitive to changes in temperature and pH. Extreme conditions can cause the enzyme to denature (unfold), leading to a loss of its catalytic activity.

2. Holoenzyme and its Components

Many enzymes require non-protein components to be active. A complete, catalytically active enzyme is called a holoenzyme.

Figure 1: The components of a Holoenzyme.

The holoenzyme consists of two parts:

  • Apoenzyme: The protein part of the enzyme, which is catalytically inactive on its own.
  • Cofactor: The non-protein part required for activity. Cofactors can be:
    • Coenzymes: Organic molecules, often derived from vitamins (e.g., NAD+, FAD), that are loosely bound to the apoenzyme.
    • Prosthetic Groups: Organic or inorganic molecules that are tightly and permanently bound to the apoenzyme (e.g., the heme group in catalase).
    • Metal Ions: Inorganic ions that help in binding the substrate or participate directly in the catalytic reaction (e.g., Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺).

3. Enzyme Nomenclature (EC Number)

To standardize the naming of enzymes, the International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB) established the Enzyme Commission (EC). Each enzyme is assigned a unique four-digit EC number.

For example, ATP:glucose phosphotransferase has the EC number EC 2.7.1.1.

  • EC 2: The first digit represents the major class (Transferase).
  • EC 2.7: The second digit indicates the subclass (transferring phosphorus-containing groups).
  • EC 2.7.1: The third digit specifies the sub-subclass (a hydroxyl group acts as the acceptor).
  • EC 2.7.1.1: The fourth digit is a serial number for the specific enzyme (glucose is the phosphate acceptor).

4. The 6 Major Classes of Enzymes

1. Oxidoreductases (EC 1)

Catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions. Example: Lactate dehydrogenase.

2. Transferases (EC 2)

Transfer functional groups (e.g., an amino or phosphate group) from one molecule to another. Example: Hexokinase.

3. Hydrolases (EC 3)

Catalyze hydrolysis, breaking bonds by adding water. Example: Trypsin.

4. Lyases (EC 4)

Break various chemical bonds by means other than hydrolysis and oxidation, often forming a new double bond. Example: Aldolase.

5. Isomerases (EC 5)

Catalyze the rearrangement of atoms within a molecule, converting one isomer to another. Example: Triose-phosphate isomerase.

6. Ligases (EC 6)

Join two molecules together, usually with the hydrolysis of ATP. Example: DNA Ligase.

5 & 6. Enzymes vs. Inorganic Catalysts

Similarities

  • Increase the rate of reaction without being consumed.
  • Required in very small quantities.
  • Do not alter the equilibrium constant of the reaction.
  • Form unstable intermediate complexes with reactants.

Differences

  • Composition: Enzymes are complex proteins; catalysts are simple inorganic molecules.
  • Specificity: Enzymes are highly specific; catalysts are often non-specific.
  • Conditions: Enzymes work under mild conditions (temp, pH); catalysts often require high temp/pressure.
  • Regulation: Enzyme activity is highly regulated in cells; catalyst activity is not.
  • Efficiency: Enzymes are generally much more efficient than inorganic catalysts.

7. Types of Enzyme Specificity

Enzyme specificity refers to their ability to catalyze only one or a limited range of chemical reactions. This is a hallmark of their function.

1. Absolute Specificity

The enzyme catalyzes only one specific reaction on one specific substrate. Example: Urease acts only on urea.

2. Group Specificity

The enzyme acts on a group of substrates that have a similar functional group or structure. Example: Pepsin cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic amino acids.

3. Linkage Specificity

The enzyme acts on a particular type of chemical bond, irrespective of the rest of the molecular structure. Example: Amylase cleaves α-1,4 glycosidic bonds in starch and glycogen.

4. Stereochemical Specificity

The enzyme acts on a particular stereoisomer (e.g., D- or L-isomer) of a substrate. Example: L-amino acid oxidase acts only on L-amino acids, not D-amino acids.
