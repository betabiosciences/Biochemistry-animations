🔄 Glyoxylate Cycle

The glyoxylate cycle is a metabolic pathway that allows organisms to convert acetyl-CoA into succinate and glyoxylate, bypassing the decarboxylation steps of the citric acid cycle. This pathway is crucial for converting fatty acids into carbohydrates, particularly in plants, bacteria, and fungi.
🎯 Primary Function
Enables the net conversion of acetyl-CoA (from fatty acid oxidation) into four-carbon compounds that can be used for gluconeogenesis, allowing organisms to synthesize glucose from fats.

Key Steps

1. Acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate (via citrate synthase)
2. Citrate is converted to isocitrate (via aconitase)
3. Isocitrate is cleaved into succinate and glyoxylate (via isocitrate lyase)
4. Glyoxylate condenses with another acetyl-CoA to form malate (via malate synthase)
5. Malate is oxidized to oxaloacetate (via malate dehydrogenase)
Key Features
Bypasses CO₂-producing steps of citric acid cycle
Net production of succinate from two acetyl-CoA molecules
Essential for seed germination in plants
Occurs in specialized organelles called glyoxysomes
Absent in most animals, including mammals

🔄 Cori Cycle

The Cori cycle is a metabolic pathway that connects muscle and liver metabolism during periods of high energy demand. It allows the conversion of lactate produced in muscles back to glucose in the liver, which can then return to muscles for energy production.
🎯 Primary Function
Prevents lactate accumulation in muscles during intense exercise and maintains blood glucose levels by recycling lactate through the liver via gluconeogenesis.

Cycle Process

1. Muscle glycolysis produces lactate during anaerobic respiration
2. Lactate is released into bloodstream and transported to liver
3. Liver converts lactate back to pyruvate (via lactate dehydrogenase)
4. Pyruvate undergoes gluconeogenesis to form glucose
5. Glucose is released into blood and returns to muscles
6. Muscles can use glucose for energy via glycolysis
Clinical Significance
Prevents lactic acidosis during intense exercise
Maintains glucose homeostasis
Energy cost: 6 ATP to convert 2 lactate to 1 glucose
Critical during fasting states
Impaired in liver disease

🔄 Glucose-Alanine Cycle

The glucose-alanine cycle is a metabolic pathway that transports amino groups from muscle to liver while simultaneously providing glucose back to muscle. This cycle plays a crucial role in nitrogen disposal and glucose homeostasis during periods of protein catabolism.
🎯 Primary Function
Safely transports ammonia from peripheral tissues (especially muscle) to the liver for urea synthesis while providing glucose to maintain energy metabolism in muscles.

Cycle Mechanism

1. Muscle proteins are catabolized, producing amino acids
2. Amino acids undergo transamination with pyruvate to form alanine
3. Alanine is released into bloodstream and transported to liver
4. Liver deaminates alanine, producing pyruvate and ammonia
5. Ammonia enters urea cycle for safe nitrogen disposal
6. Pyruvate undergoes gluconeogenesis to produce glucose
7. Glucose returns to muscle via bloodstream
Physiological Importance
Major pathway during fasting and starvation
Prevents ammonia toxicity in peripheral tissues
Alanine is the major gluconeogenic amino acid
Enhanced during muscle wasting conditions
Links protein and carbohydrate metabolism
Critical for maintaining blood glucose during prolonged exercise
