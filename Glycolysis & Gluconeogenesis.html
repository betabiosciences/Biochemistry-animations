Gluconeogenesis Pathway

Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. It occurs mainly in the liver and to a lesser extent in the kidneys.

1. Pyruvate to Phosphoenolpyruvate
Pyruvate is converted to oxaloacetate by pyruvate carboxylase (requires ATP and biotin), then to phosphoenolpyruvate by PEP carboxykinase (requires GTP). This bypasses the irreversible step of glycolysis.
Pyruvate
ATP → ADP, GTP → GDP
Pyruvate Carboxylase, PEP Carboxykinase
Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)
2. Reverse Glycolysis Steps
Phosphoenolpyruvate undergoes reversal of glycolytic steps through 2-phosphoglycerate, 3-phosphoglycerate, and 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. These reactions are catalyzed by the same enzymes as glycolysis but in reverse.
Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)
Enolase, Phosphoglycerate Mutase, Phosphoglycerate Kinase, GAP Dehydrogenase
Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate (G3P)
3. Fructose-1,6-Bisphosphate to Fructose-6-Phosphate
Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is converted to fructose-6-phosphate by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase. This bypasses the phosphofructokinase step of glycolysis, which is irreversible.

4. Glucose-6-Phosphate to Glucose
Glucose-6-phosphate is converted to glucose by glucose-6-phosphatase. This bypasses the hexokinase step of glycolysis, which is irreversible. Glucose is then released into the bloodstream.

Key Features of Gluconeogenesis

  • • Occurs primarily in the liver (90%) and kidneys (10%)
  • • Requires 6 ATP equivalents to synthesize one glucose molecule from pyruvate
  • • Uses 7 of the 10 glycolytic enzymes operating in reverse
  • • Bypasses three irreversible steps of glycolysis with different enzymes
  • • Precursors include lactate, glycerol, glucogenic amino acids, and pyruvate
  • • Regulated by hormones (glucagon, cortisol, epinephrine) and allosteric effectors
  • • Critical for maintaining blood glucose during fasting or exercise

Radiolabeling Carbons in Glycolysis

Radiolabeling experiments using 14C or 13C isotopes help trace the fate of individual carbon atoms through metabolic pathways. This technique is particularly useful for understanding carbon atom rearrangement in glycolysis.

Experiment Overview

In a typical experiment, glucose labeled with 14C at a specific carbon position is introduced to a cell system. After glycolysis, the resulting pyruvate is analyzed to determine which carbon atoms contain the radioactivity.

Glucose with Radiolabeled Carbon
1 2 3 4 5 6 → Glycolysis → 1 2 3 (Pyruvate)

Key Findings

  • • C1 and C6 of glucose become the methyl carbon of pyruvate
  • • C2 and C5 of glucose become the carbonyl carbon of pyruvate
  • • C3 and C4 of glucose become the carboxyl carbon of pyruvate
  • • This pattern results from the symmetric cleavage of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate

Applications

  • • Tracing metabolic flux in cells
  • • Studying enzyme mechanisms and carbon rearrangements
  • • Investigating metabolic diseases
  • • Measuring rates of glycolysis in different tissues

Experimental Results

When glucose labeled at C1 is metabolized, the radioactivity appears in the methyl group (C3) of pyruvate. Similarly, glucose labeled at C6 also labels the methyl group of pyruvate. This symmetry results from the division of the 6-carbon fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into two 3-carbon units that are isomers of each other.
