📚 Key Concepts

🔗 Configuration

The fixed arrangement of atoms that can only be changed by breaking and reforming covalent bonds. Represents the permanent structural framework.

🌀 Conformation

The spatial arrangement of atoms that can be changed by rotation around single bonds without breaking covalent bonds. Represents flexible 3D shapes.

🧪 Primary Structure Definition

Primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain, connected by peptide bonds. It represents the configuration of the protein - the fundamental blueprint that determines all higher levels of structure.

🔍 Key Features

📝 Sequence Specificity

Each protein has a unique amino acid sequence determined by genetic code

🔗 Covalent Bonds

Amino acids linked by strong peptide bonds (amide bonds)

➡️ Directionality

Written from N-terminus (amino end) to C-terminus (carboxyl end)

🧬 Information Content

Contains all information needed for protein folding and function

🔬 The Atomic Dance of Resonance

Imagine you're zooming into a protein at the atomic level — so close that you can see carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen atoms dancing in place. As you watch, something curious happens at each peptide bond — the invisible glue linking amino acids together.

You expect this bond — formed between a carbonyl group (C=O) and an amine group (NH₂) — to behave like a simple single bond. But this bond is no ordinary connection. It's part of a resonance effect that locks it into a unique shape.

⚡ The Electron Dance

Deep in the electron clouds, the nitrogen's lone pair isn't content to sit still. It's drawn toward the neighboring carbonyl carbon — which is slightly desperate, burdened with a δ+ charge because its oxygen neighbor hogs electrons. So the nitrogen donates its lone pair into the carbonyl system, creating resonance.

At first glance, the peptide bond looks like this:

O=C–N–H

Initial Structure

But the nitrogen's lone pair creates:

⁻O–C=N⁺–H

Resonance Structure

Neither form fully takes over ↔ Resonance Hybrid

The real structure is a mix of both, making the peptide bond stronger, flatter, and more rigid than expected.

🎭 What Resonance Does to the Peptide Bond
1. 🔒 Rigid and Planar

The C–N bond now has partial double bond character. This locks the atoms (C, O, N, H) into a flat, planar configuration — like dancers moving in unison on a molecular stage.

📏 Bond length: C–N ≈ 1.32 Å (between typical single and double bond lengths)
2. 🚫 No Twisting Allowed

Most single bonds twist and rotate like swivels. But not this one. The peptide bond becomes stubbornly rigid, resisting rotation — which is crucial in how proteins fold.

💡 Key Point: This rigidity is why proteins only bend at specific points (φ and ψ angles).
3. ⚡ Built-in Dipole

The resonance creates a strong dipole moment:

The oxygen becomes electron-rich (δ–)
The amide hydrogen becomes electron-poor (δ+)

This makes peptide bonds attractive — literally. They form hydrogen bonds that drive the helices and sheets in protein structures.

4. 🛡️ Enhanced Stability

Thanks to this electron delocalization, the peptide bond is surprisingly resistant to hydrolysis. It takes strong enzymes — like proteases — or extreme conditions to break it.
