Initiation of Prokaryotic Transcription

The Stages of Initiation

Transcription initiation is the process where the RNA polymerase (RNAP) holoenzyme binds to a specific DNA sequence called a promoter, unwinds the DNA to form a "transcription bubble," and begins synthesizing a short RNA molecule. It is the primary point of regulation for gene expression.

This intricate process doesn't happen in one step. It involves a series of conformational changes in both the polymerase and the DNA, progressing through several distinct structural intermediates, which we will explore one by one.

1. Closed Promoter Complex (RCC)

The first step is the reversible binding of the RNAP holoenzyme (the core enzyme plus a specificity sigma factor, e.g., σ⁷⁰) to the promoter. This forms the Closed Promoter Complex (RCC).

  • State: The promoter DNA is still fully double-stranded (hence "closed").
  • Mechanism: This binding is primarily driven by the sigma (σ) factor recognizing and binding to key promoter elements, such as the -35 and -10 boxes.
  • Nature: This initial complex is relatively unstable and reversible. The polymerase is "scanning" or "searching" for its target. The equilibrium constant (KB) is typically in the range of 10⁶ to 10⁹ M⁻¹.

2. The Intermediate Stage

Between the closed and open complexes lies a short-lived, transient Intermediate Stage. This is not a stable "stop" but rather the *process* of isomerization.

  • Conformational Change: This stage involves significant structural changes in both the RNAP and the DNA. The DNA, often at the AT-rich -10 region, begins to bend and "kink."
  • Rate-Limiting: This transition, often called isomerization, is typically the rate-limiting step of initiation. It's the "point of no return."

3. Open Promoter Complex (RPO)

This is the "go" signal. The Open Promoter Complex (RPO) is a stable, long-lived complex where the DNA is locally unwound.

  • State: A "transcription bubble" of ~12-14 base pairs is formed, spanning from approximately -11 to +2 or +3 relative to the transcription start site (+1).
  • Mechanism: This "melting" is achieved without ATP hydrolysis. The non-template strand is stabilized by the σ factor, while the template strand is threaded into the RNAP active site.
  • Competence: The complex is now "transcriptionally competent." The active site is properly assembled, and the template strand is ready to be read. This complex is, for all practical purposes, irreversible.

4. The Scrunched Complex

Once the RPO is formed, RNAP begins synthesizing the first few ribonucleotides. However, the polymerase itself remains stationary on the promoter. This creates a stressed intermediate called the Scrunched Complex.

  • Mechanism: As RNAP synthesizes the nascent RNA chain, it pulls downstream DNA *into* the complex without moving its "front" end.
  • Analogy: Imagine pulling a rope towards you while your feet are glued to the floor. The rope (DNA) bunches up in your arms (within the polymerase).
  • Stored Energy: This "scrunching" builds up considerable potential energy within the complex. This energy is crucial for the next steps.

5. Abortive Initiation

Abortive Initiation is a "stuttering" process where the scrunched complex repeatedly synthesizes and then releases short RNA transcripts (typically 2-15 nucleotides) without leaving the promoter.

  • Common Outcome: This is the most common fate of an initial transcribing complex. Many abortive cycles can occur before one successful "escape."
  • Mechanism: The nascent RNA chain "collides" with a part of the σ factor (specifically region 3.2) that partially blocks the RNA exit channel.
  • Reset: The scrunch energy is released, the short RNA transcript dissociates, and the polymerase resets to the RPO state, ready to try again.

6. Promoter Clearance

Promoter Clearance is the "checkpoint" that a transcribing complex must pass to end the abortive cycle. It's the event of successfully synthesizing an RNA transcript that is long enough to avoid being aborted.

  • Critical Length: This typically occurs when the nascent RNA chain reaches a length of ~10-15 nucleotides.
  • Mechanism: At this length, the growing RNA chain is now "strong" enough to displace the σ factor's 3.2 loop from the RNA exit channel.
  • Stability: This displacement creates a more stable ternary complex (DNA-RNA-Polymerase) and "clears" the path for the RNA to exit. The complex is now poised to escape.

7. Promoter Escape

Promoter Escape is the climax of initiation. It is the physical transition from a stationary, promoter-bound complex to a mobile, processive elongation complex.

  • Energy Release: The polymerase uses the stored "scrunch" energy to break its strong tethers to the promoter elements (both upstream and downstream).
  • Key Break: This includes breaking the strong interactions between the σ factor and the -10 and -35 boxes, as well as core enzyme contacts.
  • Movement: The polymerase "leaps" forward, its "front" end (downstream clamp) finally moving down the DNA. The transcription bubble moves with it.

8. The Transcription Elongation Complex (TEC)

Once promoter escape is complete, the Transcription Elongation Complex (TEC) is formed. This is the final, stable, and highly processive machine that will synthesize the remainder of the gene.

  • Sigma Dissociation: The σ factor, having served its purpose for initiation, becomes less tightly bound and typically dissociates from the core enzyme.
  • Core Enzyme: The core RNAP enzyme is the engine of elongation. It maintains the transcription bubble, unwinds DNA ahead, re-anneals it behind, and polymerizes the RNA chain.
  • High Processivity: The TEC is incredibly stable, capable of synthesizing thousands of nucleotides without falling off the DNA template, moving at speeds of 50-100 nucleotides per second.
  • Factor Binding: Elongation factors (like NusA and NusG) may now bind to the core enzyme to regulate its speed, pausing, and termination.
