1. Classification of Ion Channels

Ion channels are integral membrane proteins that form pores, allowing ions to flow across cell membranes. This movement of ions is fundamental to numerous physiological processes including nerve impulse transmission, muscle contraction, and cellular signaling.

Key Concept

Ion channels are characterized by their selectivity (which ions they allow to pass) and their gating mechanism (what opens and closes them).

Classification by Gating Mechanism:

  • Voltage-gated channels - Open or close in response to changes in membrane potential. Examples include voltage-gated sodium, potassium, and calcium channels.
  • Ligand-gated channels - Open when a specific chemical messenger (ligand) binds to the channel. These include neurotransmitter receptors like nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and GABA receptors.
  • Mechanically-gated channels - Respond to physical forces such as pressure, stretch, or vibration. Found in sensory systems like hearing and touch.
  • Leak channels - Remain open at rest, contributing to the resting membrane potential. Potassium leak channels are particularly important for maintaining the resting potential.

Classification by Ion Selectivity:

  • Sodium channels (Na⁺) - Critical for action potential generation
  • Potassium channels (K⁺) - Diverse family with roles in repolarization and setting resting potential
  • Calcium channels (Ca²⁺) - Important for muscle contraction, neurotransmitter release, and second messenger signaling
  • Chloride channels (Cl⁻) - Regulate cell volume and contribute to inhibitory neurotransmission
  • Non-selective cation channels - Allow passage of several cation types, often activated by nucleotides or other intracellular signals

Note: Some channels exhibit multiple gating mechanisms. For example, certain potassium channels can be both voltage-gated and calcium-activated.

2. Potassium Leak Channels: Structure & Function

Potassium leak channels are a specialized class of ion channels that remain constitutively open at rest, allowing potassium ions to passively flow down their concentration gradient. These channels play a fundamental role in establishing and maintaining the resting membrane potential in virtually all cells.

Structural Organization:

3D structure of a potassium channel showing the pore region and selectivity filter
  • Potassium leak channels belong to the two-pore domain potassium channel (K2P) family
  • These channels are tetramers composed of subunits that each contain two pore-forming domains, unlike typical potassium channels with one per subunit.
  • Each subunit contains 4 transmembrane segments (M1-M4) and two pore-forming domains (P1 and P2)
  • Functional channels are formed by dimerization of two subunits, creating a channel with four pore domains
  • Unlike voltage-gated potassium channels, K2P channels lack a voltage-sensing domain (S4 segment)
  • The pore architecture includes a selectivity filter with the conserved GYG signature sequence that ensures potassium selectivity
  • Many K2P channels have large extracellular cap domains that may regulate channel activity

Physiological Functions:

  • Establish and maintain the resting membrane potential close to the potassium equilibrium potential (EK)
  • Provide a background potassium conductance that stabilizes the membrane potential
  • Contribute to cellular excitability by setting the baseline membrane potential
  • Participate in potassium buffering in the brain, helping to maintain extracellular potassium homeostasis
  • Mediate responses to various physical and chemical stimuli including pH, temperature, and membrane stretch
  • Play roles in anesthetic actions, neuroprotection, and regulation of vascular tone

Key Concept

Potassium leak channels are responsible for the high resting potassium permeability of cells, which keeps the membrane potential close to EK (-90 mV). The small but significant sodium permeability through sodium leak channels prevents the membrane potential from reaching exactly EK, resulting in the typical resting potential of -70 mV.

Example: TASK-1 channels in cerebellar granule neurons contribute to the resting potassium conductance and help set the input resistance of these cells, influencing their responsiveness to synaptic inputs.

3. Sodium Leak Channels: Structure & Function

Sodium leak channels, also known as background or resting sodium channels, provide a small but significant sodium permeability at rest. While less prominent than potassium leak channels, they play a crucial role in setting the precise value of the resting membrane potential.

Structural Organization:

  • Sodium leak channels belong to the NALCN (sodium leak channel, non-selective) family
  • NALCN channels share structural homology with voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels, with four homologous domains, each containing 6 transmembrane segments
  • Unlike voltage-gated sodium channels, NALCN channels have altered voltage-sensing domains that render them relatively voltage-insensitive
  • The pore region contains specific residues that confer sodium selectivity while allowing some calcium permeation
  • The channels also interact with GPCR pathways that modulate their activity

Key Properties:

  • Exhibit weak voltage dependence compared to voltage-gated sodium channels
  • Permeable to calcium ions, though with lower permeability than sodium
  • Resistant to tetrodotoxin (TTX), which blocks voltage-gated sodium channels
  • Modulated by various neurotransmitters and neuropeptides including substance P, acetylcholine, and opioids
  • Regulated by extracellular calcium concentration through a calcium-sensing receptor mechanism

Physiological Functions:

  • Contribute to the resting membrane potential by providing background sodium conductance
  • Help establish the driving force for sodium entry during action potentials and synaptic transmission
  • Play a role in respiratory rhythm generation
  • Participate in sleep-wake regulation and circadian rhythms
  • Contribute to neuronal excitability and spontaneous firing in some neuronal populations
  • Involved in locomotion and motor control circuits
  • Play roles in pain perception and modulation

Note: The sodium leak conductance is much smaller than the potassium leak conductance at rest (PK >> PNa), which is why the resting membrane potential (-70 mV) is much closer to EK (-90 mV) than to ENa (+60 mV).

Example: Mutations in the NALCN gene cause severe neurological disorders in humans, including infantile hypotonia with psychomotor retardation and characteristic facies (IHPRF syndrome), highlighting the importance of sodium leak channels in normal neuronal function.

4. Membrane Potential Concepts

The membrane potential is the electrical potential difference across the cell membrane, typically ranging from -40 to -90 mV in most cells. This electrical gradient is essential for cellular communication and function.

Equilibrium Potential (Eion):

The membrane potential at which there is no net movement of a specific ion across the membrane. At this potential, the chemical and electrical driving forces are balanced.

Key Concept

The equilibrium potential represents the point where the chemical driving force (concentration gradient) exactly balances the electrical driving force (membrane potential).

Electrochemical Driving Force (EDF):

The net driving force acting on an ion, calculated as:

EDF = Vm - Eion

Where Vm is the membrane potential and Eion is the equilibrium potential for that ion.

  • If EDF is negative, the ion will tend to move into the cell
  • If EDF is positive, the ion will tend to move out of the cell
  • If EDF is zero, there is no net movement of the ion

Chemical Driving Force (CDF):

The force resulting from concentration gradients across the membrane. Ions tend to move from areas of high concentration to areas of low concentration.

Example: For potassium ions (K⁺), the intracellular concentration is typically around 140 mM while extracellular concentration is about 5 mM. This creates a strong chemical driving force for K⁺ to move out of the cell.

Resting Membrane Potential:

The steady-state membrane potential when the cell is not actively signaling. It is primarily determined by:

  • The unequal distribution of ions across the membrane
  • The relative permeability of the membrane to different ions
  • The activity of ion pumps (especially Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase)

5. Nernst Equation & Equilibrium Potential

The Nernst equation calculates the equilibrium potential for a specific ion based on its concentration gradient across the membrane. This fundamental equation links chemical gradients to electrical potentials.

Nernst Equation:

Eion = (RT/zF) × ln([ion]out / [ion]in)

Where:

  • Eion = equilibrium potential for the ion
  • R = universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K)
  • T = absolute temperature in Kelvin
  • z = valence (charge) of the ion
  • F = Faraday's constant (96,485 C/mol)
  • [ion]out = extracellular ion concentration
  • [ion]in = intracellular ion concentration

Simplified Nernst Equation (at 37°C):

Eion = (61.5 / z) × log([ion]out / [ion]in) mV

Example Calculation for K⁺:

Typical concentrations: [K⁺]out = 5 mM, [K⁺]in = 140 mM

EK = (61.5 / 1) × log(5 / 140) = 61.5 × log(0.0357) ≈ -90 mV

Example Calculation for Na⁺:

Typical concentrations: [Na⁺]out = 145 mM, [Na⁺]in = 15 mM

ENa = (61.5 / 1) × log(145 / 15) = 61.5 × log(9.67) ≈ +60 mV

Note: The Nernst equation assumes the membrane is permeable to only one ion at a time. In reality, multiple ions contribute to the membrane potential simultaneously.

6. Significance of Na⁺ and K⁺ Equilibrium Potentials

The equilibrium potentials for sodium and potassium ions are critically important for understanding electrical signaling in cells. Their values determine the direction and magnitude of ion flow during changes in membrane permeability.

Potassium Equilibrium Potential (EK):

  • Typically around -90 mV in mammalian cells
  • Close to the resting membrane potential of most cells (-70 mV)
  • K⁺ leak channels keep the membrane potential near EK
  • Changes in extracellular K⁺ concentration significantly affect resting potential
  • Hyperkalemia (high blood K⁺) depolarizes cells, while hypokalemia hyperpolarizes them

Sodium Equilibrium Potential (ENa):

  • Typically around +60 mV in mammalian cells
  • Far from the resting membrane potential
  • Creates a strong driving force for Na⁺ influx when Na⁺ channels open
  • Responsible for the upstroke of action potentials
  • Small changes in extracellular Na⁺ have minimal effect on resting potential but significantly impact action potential amplitude

Physiological Significance:

The large difference between ENa and EK creates the electrochemical gradient necessary for:

  • Generation of action potentials with rapid depolarization and repolarization
  • Maintenance of resting membrane potential
  • Secondary active transport processes (e.g., glucose absorption)
  • Cell volume regulation through coordinated movement of ions and water

Example: During an action potential, sodium channels open, and the membrane potential moves toward ENa (+60 mV). Then potassium channels open, and the membrane potential moves back toward EK (-90 mV), often briefly hyperpolarizing before returning to the resting potential.

Ion Typical Eion Effect on Membrane Potential Primary Channel Types
K⁺ -90 mV Hyperpolarizing Leak, voltage-gated, Ca²⁺-activated
Na⁺ +60 mV Depolarizing Voltage-gated, epithelial
Ca²⁺ +120 mV Strongly depolarizing Voltage-gated, ligand-gated
Cl⁻ -65 mV Stabilizing (near resting potential) Ligand-gated, volume-regulated

7. Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz Equation

The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) equation calculates the membrane potential when multiple ions contribute to the permeability of the membrane. It provides a more accurate prediction of membrane potential than the Nernst equation for real cells.

GHK Voltage Equation:

Vm = (RT/F) × ln( (PK[K⁺]out + PNa[Na⁺]out + PCl[Cl⁻]in) / (PK[K⁺]in + PNa[Na⁺]in + PCl[Cl⁻]out) )

Where Pion represents the membrane permeability to each ion.

Key Points:

  • The GHK equation accounts for multiple permeant ions simultaneously
  • The resting membrane potential is closest to the equilibrium potential of the most permeable ion
  • At rest, PK >> PNa, so Vm is close to EK
  • During an action potential, PNa increases dramatically, driving Vm toward ENa
  • Chloride ions (Cl⁻) often distribute passively according to the membrane potential

Simplified Version (for K⁺ and Na⁺ only):

Vm = (RT/F) × ln( (PK[K⁺]out + PNa[Na⁺]out) / (PK[K⁺]in + PNa[Na⁺]in) )

Example Calculation:

Using typical concentrations and relative permeabilities at rest (PK:PNa = 1:0.04):

Vm = 61.5 × log( (1×5 + 0.04×145) / (1×140 + 0.04×15) ) ≈ -70 mV

Key Concept

The GHK equation explains why the resting membrane potential (-70 mV) is slightly more positive than EK (-90 mV) due to the small but significant sodium permeability at rest.

8. Voltage-Gated Channels

Voltage-gated channels open and close in response to changes in membrane potential. They are fundamental to electrical signaling in excitable cells like neurons and muscle cells.

Voltage-Gated Sodium Channels (Nav):

Structure of voltage-gated sodium channel showing voltage-sensing domains
  • Rapid activation (within 0.1-0.2 ms) and fast inactivation
  • Responsible for the rising phase of action potentials
  • Four domains, each with 6 transmembrane segments
  • S4 segments act as voltage sensors with positive charges
  • Inactivation mediated by the "ball and chain" mechanism (III-IV linker)
  • Local anesthetics (e.g., lidocaine) block these channels by binding to the inner pore

Three Functional States of Voltage-Gated Sodium Channels:

Three states of voltage-gated sodium channels: closed, open, and inactivated
Closed State
-70 mV to -90 mV

Channel is non-conducting but capable of opening. Activation gate closed, inactivation gate open.

Open State
-55 mV to +30 mV

Channel is conducting, allowing Na⁺ influx. Both activation and inactivation gates are open.

Inactive State
> -55 mV (depolarized)

Channel is non-conducting and refractory. Activation gate may be open, but inactivation gate is closed.

Detailed State Transitions:

  • Closed → Open: Occurs when membrane depolarizes to threshold potential (-55 mV). Voltage sensors (S4 segments) move outward, causing conformational changes that open the activation gate.
  • Open → Inactive: Occurs automatically 1-2 ms after opening. The intracellular loop between domains III and IV (the "inactivation gate") blocks the pore.
  • Inactive → Closed: Requires membrane repolarization to below -70 mV. The inactivation gate swings away from the pore, allowing the channel to return to the closed state.

Voltage-Gated Potassium Channels (Kv):

  • Slower activation than Na⁺ channels (1-2 ms)
  • No fast inactivation (except for A-type K⁺ channels)
  • Responsible for repolarization and hyperpolarization
  • Four subunits, each with 6 transmembrane segments
  • Diversity in Kv channels allows for fine-tuning of electrical signaling
  • Some Kv channels inactivate via N-type (ball and chain) or C-type (pore collapse) mechanisms

Gating Mechanism:

Voltage sensing involves:

  1. Movement of charged S4 segments in response to membrane potential changes
  2. Conformational changes that open the pore
  3. Inactivation mechanisms that close the channel during sustained depolarization

Voltage-Gated Calcium Channels (Cav):

  • Similar structure to Nav channels but with different selectivity
  • Critical for neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction, and gene expression
  • Slower activation and inactivation than Nav channels
  • Classified into L-, N-, P/Q-, R-, and T-types based on biophysical and pharmacological properties

Key Concept

The three states of voltage-gated sodium channels create the refractory periods that prevent backward propagation of action potentials and limit their frequency. The absolute refractory period corresponds to when channels are inactivated, while the relative refractory period occurs during recovery when some channels are still inactivated.

Example: The sequential opening of voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels generates the action potential: Na⁺ channel opening causes rapid depolarization, while delayed K⁺ channel opening repolarizes the membrane. The inactivation of Na⁺ channels ensures the action potential moves in one direction along the axon.

9. Ligand-Gated Ion Channels

Ligand-gated channels open when a specific chemical messenger (ligand) binds to the channel. They mediate fast synaptic transmission and are targets for many drugs.

Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor (nAChR):

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor structure and activation mechanism
  • Pentameric structure with five subunits (α, β, γ, δ, ε) arranged around a central pore
  • Each subunit has 4 transmembrane domains (M1-M4)
  • Binding of two acetylcholine molecules opens the channel
  • Permeable to Na⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ (non-selective cation channel)
  • Found at neuromuscular junctions and in the CNS
  • Agonists: nicotine, acetylcholine; Antagonists: curare, α-bungarotoxin

Mechanism of Action:

  1. Acetylcholine binds to extracellular sites on α-subunits
  2. Conformational change opens the channel pore
  3. Cations flow through, depolarizing the membrane (excitatory effect)
  4. Channel closes when acetylcholine dissociates or is degraded by acetylcholinesterase

Other Ligand-Gated Channels:

  • GABAA receptors - Cl⁻ permeable, inhibitory, targeted by benzodiazepines and barbiturates
  • Glycine receptors - Cl⁻ permeable, inhibitory, mainly in spinal cord
  • Ionotropic glutamate receptors - Cation permeable, excitatory, include AMPA, NMDA, and kainate receptors
  • ATP-gated P2X receptors - Cation permeable, involved in pain signaling
  • 5-HT3 receptors - Cation permeable, excitatory serotonin receptors

Key Concept

Ligand-gated channels mediate fast synaptic transmission (millisecond timescale), while G-protein coupled receptors mediate slow synaptic transmission (seconds to minutes).

Receptor Type Primary Ions Effect Main Locations
nAChR Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ Excitatory Neuromuscular junction, CNS
GABAA Cl⁻ Inhibitory CNS (widespread)
AMPA Na⁺, K⁺ Excitatory CNS (glutamatergic synapses)

10. Membrane Potential Measurement

Glass microelectrodes are used to measure membrane potential in individual cells. This technique, developed in the mid-20th century, revolutionized our understanding of cellular electrophysiology.

Glass Microelectrode Technique:

Setup for measuring membrane potential using intracellular microelectrodes
  • Fine-tipped glass pipettes filled with conductive solution (usually 3M KCl)
  • Tip diameter typically 0.5-1.0 μm
  • High electrical resistance (10-100 MΩ)
  • Connected to a high-impedance amplifier (10¹² Ω input impedance)
  • Ag/AgCl wires provide stable electrical connection

Measurement Procedure:

  1. Microelectrode is advanced toward the cell using a micromanipulator
  2. Upon penetration of the cell membrane, a sudden negative potential is recorded
  3. This potential represents the resting membrane potential
  4. Changes in potential during electrical activity can be recorded
  5. Intracellular injection of dyes or substances is possible through the pipette

Advantages and Limitations:

  • Advantages: Direct measurement, high temporal resolution, intracellular access for manipulation
  • Limitations: Cell damage during impalement, difficult long-term recordings, limited spatial resolution, cannot measure very small cells

Alternative Techniques:

  • Voltage-sensitive dyes - Fluorescent molecules that change emission with membrane potential
  • Patch clamp - Higher resolution technique (see next section)
  • Field potential recording - Extracellular recording of population activity
  • Optogenetics - Using light-sensitive channels to control and monitor membrane potential

Example: Using intracellular microelectrodes, Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley characterized the ionic basis of the action potential in squid giant axons, work for which they received the Nobel Prize in 1963.

11. Patch Clamp Principles

The patch clamp technique, developed by Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann (Nobel Prize 1991), revolutionized cellular physiology by enabling recording of ionic currents through individual ion channels. This powerful method provides unprecedented insight into channel properties and regulation.

Patch clamp recording of single ion channel currents showing open and closed states

Fundamental Principles:

  • Uses a glass micropipette with a tip diameter of ~1 μm to form a high-resistance seal (gigaohm seal) with the cell membrane
  • Allows recording of extremely small currents in the picoampere (10⁻¹² A) range
  • Can measure single channel activity or whole-cell currents
  • Operates in voltage clamp mode to control membrane potential while measuring current, or current clamp mode to record membrane potential changes
  • Requires specialized equipment including a vibration-isolation table, Faraday cage, and high-gain low-noise amplifier

Patch Configurations:

  1. Cell-attached - Pipette sealed to intact cell, non-invasive recording of channel activity without disrupting intracellular environment
  2. Whole-cell - Membrane patch ruptured after seal formation, allowing electrical and diffusional access to the cell interior
  3. Inside-out - Excised patch with cytoplasmic side exposed to bath solution, ideal for studying regulation by intracellular factors
  4. Outside-out - Excised patch with extracellular side exposed, perfect for studying effects of extracellular ligands

Applications and Impact:

  • Characterization of single channel properties (conductance, gating kinetics, open probability)
  • Pharmacological profiling of ion channels and drug discovery
  • Investigation of channel regulation by second messengers, phosphorylation, and interacting proteins
  • Elucidation of disease mechanisms involving ion channel dysfunction (channelopathies)
  • Study of synaptic transmission and presynaptic mechanisms

Technical Considerations

Successful patch clamping requires meticulous technique: clean solutions, properly fabricated pipettes, vibration isolation, and careful manipulation. The quality of the seal (resistance >1 GΩ) critically determines signal-to-noise ratio.

Example: Patch clamp studies revealed how cystic fibrosis results from defective CFTR chloride channels, leading to the development of drugs that specifically target these channel defects.
