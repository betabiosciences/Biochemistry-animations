Amino Acids Structure

General Structure of Amino Acids

Key Components

  • Alpha Carbon (Cα): Central carbon atom to which all other groups are attached
  • Amino Group (NH₂): Basic group that can accept a proton to form NH₃⁺
  • Carboxyl Group (COOH): Acidic group that can donate a proton to form COO⁻
  • R Group: Side chain that gives each amino acid its unique properties
  • Hydrogen: Attached to the alpha carbon

Zwitterionic Form

At physiological pH, amino acids exist as zwitterions, where the amino group is protonated (NH₃⁺) and the carboxyl group is deprotonated (COO⁻).

Chirality

Most amino acids are chiral molecules because the alpha carbon is bonded to four different groups. This creates L and D stereoisomers, with L-amino acids being the predominant form in proteins.

Average Amino Acid Weight

The average molecular weight of an amino acid in a protein is approximately 128 Daltons (Da). However, when considering the formation of a polypeptide chain, a water molecule is removed during peptide bond formation. Therefore, the average residue weight (amino acid within a polypeptide) is closer to 110 Da.

Note: A Dalton (Da), also known as an atomic mass unit (amu), is a unit of mass used to express atomic and molecular weights. One Dalton is approximately equal to the mass of one proton or one neutron.

Chirality in Amino Acids

Chirality, or "handedness," is a fundamental property in organic chemistry and biochemistry. A molecule is chiral if it is non-superimposable on its mirror image. This concept is crucial for understanding the specificity of biological interactions.

Concept of Chirality

A carbon atom is considered a chiral center (or stereocenter) if it is bonded to four different groups. In amino acids, the alpha-carbon (Cα) is typically a chiral center, as it is bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a unique R-group. The only exception is Glycine, where the R-group is also a hydrogen atom, making its alpha-carbon attached to two identical groups, thus rendering it achiral.

These non-superimposable mirror images are called enantiomers. Enantiomers have identical physical properties (like melting point, boiling point, solubility) but differ in their interaction with plane-polarized light (rotating it in opposite directions) and their biological activity.

Stereoisomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula and sequence of bonded atoms (constitution) but differ in the three-dimensional orientations of their atoms in space. Enantiomers are a specific type of stereoisomer.

Number of Chiral Centers in Amino Acids

Most standard amino acids have one chiral center, which is the alpha-carbon (Cα). However, there are notable exceptions:

  • Glycine: As mentioned, Glycine is achiral because its R-group is a hydrogen atom, meaning its Cα is bonded to two identical hydrogen atoms.
  • Isoleucine and Threonine: These two amino acids are unique among the common 20 because they possess two chiral centers. In addition to the alpha-carbon, each has another chiral carbon within its R-group. This means they can exist as four possible stereoisomers (2n, where n is the number of chiral centers).

Types of Amino Acids based on Chirality

  • L-Amino Acids: In biological systems, almost all amino acids found in proteins are of the L-configuration. The L/D designation refers to the stereochemistry around the alpha-carbon, specifically the orientation of the amino group. In a Fischer projection, the amino group in an L-amino acid is on the left.
  • D-Amino Acids: These are the mirror images of L-amino acids. While rare in proteins, D-amino acids are found in bacterial cell walls and some peptide antibiotics.
