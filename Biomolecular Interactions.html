Protein Sequencing: A Deep Dive

Introduction to Protein Sequencing

Protein sequencing is the process of determining the amino acid sequence of a protein, also known as its primary structure. This information is crucial for understanding protein function, structure, evolution, and for identifying post-translational modifications. Historically, this involved laborious chemical and enzymatic methods, but modern techniques, particularly mass spectrometry, have revolutionized the field.

1. Protein Purification and Quantification

Before any sequencing can commence, the protein of interest must be isolated and purified from a complex biological mixture. The level of purity required depends on the sequencing method, but generally, high purity is essential to avoid ambiguous results.

1.1. Protein Purification

Techniques include chromatography (ion-exchange, size-exclusion, affinity), electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, 2D-PAGE), and precipitation methods. The goal is to obtain a single protein species, free from contaminating proteins and other macromolecules.

1.2. Amino Acid Quantification

Accurate quantification of the purified protein is critical for downstream applications, especially for methods requiring stoichiometric amounts of reagents.

Bradford Assay: A colorimetric protein assay based on the absorbance shift of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 dye upon binding to protein. It's fast and sensitive but can be affected by detergents.

Lowry Assay: A two-step process. First, protein reacts with copper ions in alkaline solution. Second, the copper-protein complex reduces Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (phosphomolybdic-phosphotungsten acid) to produce a blue color. It's highly sensitive but time-consuming and prone to interference from various chemicals (e.g., reducing agents, detergents).

UV Absorbance (A280): Proteins absorb UV light at 280 nm due to the presence of aromatic amino acids (Tryptophan, Tyrosine, and Phenylalanine). This method is rapid and non-destructive but less sensitive and requires a pure protein with known extinction coefficient.

2. Amino Acid Composition Analysis

Before determining the sequence, it's often useful to know the amino acid composition. This involves complete hydrolysis of the protein into its constituent amino acids, followed by their separation and quantification.

2.1. Hydrolysis

The most common method is acid hydrolysis using 6M HCl at 110°C for 24-72 hours. This breaks all peptide bonds. However, Tryptophan is destroyed, and Serine, Threonine, and Tyrosine are partially degraded. Cysteine and Cystine are also destroyed and require performic acid oxidation prior to hydrolysis to convert them to stable cysteic acid.

Alkaline hydrolysis can be used for Tryptophan, but it degrades other amino acids. Enzymatic hydrolysis (e.g., using pronase) is milder but often incomplete.

2.2. Derivatization and Separation

After hydrolysis, the free amino acids are typically derivatized to make them more volatile or detectable. Common derivatizing agents include:

  • Phenylisothiocyanate (PITC): Forms Phenylthiocarbamyl (PTC) amino acids, which are stable and can be separated by Reverse-Phase HPLC.
  • o-Phthalaldehyde (OPA): Reacts with primary amines to form highly fluorescent derivatives, detected by HPLC with fluorescence detection. Proline (a secondary amine) does not react and requires pre-column derivatization with another reagent or post-column derivatization.

The derivatized amino acids are then separated using techniques like High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) or Gas Chromatography (GC), and quantified based on their peak areas compared to known standards.

3. N-terminal and C-terminal Determination

Identifying the N-terminal (amino-terminal) and C-terminal (carboxyl-terminal) amino acids provides crucial information, especially for proteins with multiple subunits or for confirming protein integrity.

3.1. N-terminal Determination: Sanger Method

Developed by Frederick Sanger, this was one of the earliest methods for N-terminal analysis.

Sanger Method Principle:

The N-terminal amino acid reacts with 2,4-dinitrofluorobenzene (DNFB), also known as Sanger's reagent, under mild alkaline conditions. This forms a dinitrophenyl (DNP) derivative of the N-terminal amino acid.

Reaction: DNFB + N-terminal Amino Acid $\rightarrow$ DNP-Amino Acid

After the reaction, the protein is completely hydrolyzed with 6M HCl. Only the N-terminal DNP-amino acid remains stable during hydrolysis, while all other peptide bonds are cleaved. The DNP-amino acid is then extracted with an organic solvent and identified by chromatography (e.g., paper chromatography) and comparison with known standards.

Limitations: This method is destructive to the rest of the protein and can only identify one N-terminal amino acid per protein chain. It is not suitable for sequencing beyond the first residue.

3.2. N-terminal Determination: Edman Degradation

Developed by Pehr Edman, this is a more powerful and widely used method because it allows for sequential removal and identification of amino acids from the N-terminus, enabling sequencing of up to 50-60 residues from a single protein.

Principle: The N-terminal amino acid reacts with Phenylisothiocyanate (PITC) under mild alkaline conditions to form a phenylthiocarbamyl (PTC) adduct of the N-terminal amino acid. This reaction is specific for the free $\alpha$-amino group.

Cyclization: The PTC-peptide is then treated with anhydrous trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), which cleaves the N-terminal amino acid as an anilinothiazolinone (ATZ) derivative, without hydrolyzing the other peptide bonds. This is the crucial step for sequential degradation.

Conversion: The unstable ATZ-amino acid is extracted and then converted to a more stable phenylthiohydantoin (PTH) amino acid by treatment with aqueous acid. This PTH-amino acid is the final identifiable product.

Repetition: The remaining peptide, now shortened by one amino acid, is ready for the next cycle of degradation, allowing for sequential identification of amino acids.

Automation: Edman degradation is typically performed using an automated instrument called a protein sequenator, which precisely controls reagent delivery, reaction conditions, and sample handling.

3.3. C-terminal Determination

Determining the C-terminal amino acid is generally more challenging than N-terminal analysis.

  • Carboxypeptidases: These enzymes cleave amino acids sequentially from the C-terminus. Different carboxypeptidases (e.g., Carboxypeptidase A, B, Y) have different specificities, allowing for the identification of various C-terminal residues. The rate of release of each amino acid is monitored.
  • Hydrazinolysis: Treatment with anhydrous hydrazine cleaves all peptide bonds, converting all amino acid residues into amino acid hydrazides, except for the C-terminal amino acid, which remains as a free amino acid. This free amino acid can then be identified. However, this method is harsh and can lead to side reactions.

4. Cleavage into Smaller Peptides

Most proteins are too large for direct sequencing by Edman degradation (limited to ~50-60 residues). Therefore, larger proteins must be cleaved into smaller, overlapping peptides.

4.1. Chemical Cleavage

  • Cyanogen Bromide (CNBr): Specifically cleaves peptide bonds on the C-terminal side of Methionine residues. This is a highly specific and reliable cleavage method.
  • Hydroxylamine: Cleaves Asparagine-Glycine (Asn-Gly) bonds.
  • Acid Hydrolysis (partial): Can be used to generate random fragments, but specificity is low.

4.2. Enzymatic Cleavage (Proteases)

Proteases are enzymes that hydrolyze peptide bonds at specific sites. Using different proteases generates distinct sets of peptides, which is crucial for obtaining overlapping sequences.

Trypsin: Cleaves peptide bonds at the C-terminal side of Lysine (K) and Arginine (R) residues.

Chymotrypsin: Cleaves peptide bonds at the C-terminal side of large hydrophobic amino acids, primarily Phenylalanine (F), Tryptophan (W), Tyrosine (Y), and Leucine (L).

Elastase: Cleaves peptide bonds at the C-terminal side of small neutral amino acids, primarily Alanine (A), Glycine (G), Serine (S), and Valine (V).

5. Mass Spectrometry for Protein Sequencing

While Edman degradation is a cornerstone method, modern protein sequencing heavily relies on mass spectrometry (MS) due to its high sensitivity, speed, and ability to analyze complex mixtures.

5.1. Tandem Mass Spectrometry (MS/MS)

MS/MS is the core technique. It involves two stages of mass analysis:

  • Stage 1 (MS1): A mass analyzer selects a peptide ion (the "precursor ion") of a specific mass-to-charge ratio (m/z).
  • Stage 2 (MS2): The selected precursor ion is fragmented in a collision cell. This fragmentation typically breaks the peptide bonds, generating a series of smaller "fragment ions" (e.g., b-ions and y-ions).
  • Detection: The fragment ions are then analyzed by a second mass analyzer, producing a mass spectrum of the fragments. By analyzing the mass differences between the fragment ions, the amino acid sequence can be deduced.
