Protein Supersecondary Structures

Exploring the building blocks of protein tertiary structure

Introduction to Supersecondary Structures

Supersecondary structures, also known as motifs, are recurring arrangements of secondary structure elements (alpha-helices and beta-sheets). They are larger than a single secondary structure but smaller than a whole protein domain. These motifs provide the stable structural scaffold that a protein domain is built upon. Their specific arrangement is critical for a protein's function, often serving as binding sites for other molecules or mediating protein-protein interactions.

Common Supersecondary Motifs

Several well-known motifs are found across various protein families:

  • Helix-turn-helix: Two alpha-helices joined by a short loop. This motif is a fundamental component of DNA-binding proteins.
  • Beta-hairpin: Two adjacent antiparallel beta-strands connected by a tight loop. This is one of the simplest and most common motifs.
  • Greek key motif: A more complex beta-sheet motif with four or more antiparallel strands that fold over each other in a specific pattern. It's named for its resemblance to the Greek key design.
  • Beta-alpha-beta motif: Two parallel beta-strands are linked by an alpha-helix, forming a crucial connection within a protein's structure.

The Role of Motifs in Protein Folding

Motifs are believed to form early during the protein folding process. They act as "nucleation sites," providing a stable starting point around which the rest of the protein's structure can assemble. The formation of these motifs reduces the conformational search space for the folding polypeptide chain, allowing the protein to reach its final, functional tertiary structure more efficiently.
