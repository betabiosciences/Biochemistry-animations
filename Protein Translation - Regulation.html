Regulation of Protein Translation

Section 1 of 8

1. Translational Coupling

Translational coupling is a regulatory mechanism where the translation of one gene affects the translation initiation of a downstream gene. This typically occurs in prokaryotic operons where multiple genes are transcribed as a single mRNA molecule.

In this mechanism, the translation of an upstream open reading frame (ORF) influences the accessibility of the ribosome binding site (RBS) for the downstream ORF. When the upstream ORF is translated, the ribosome may disrupt secondary structures that would otherwise sequester the downstream RBS, allowing translation to initiate.

Translational coupling ensures coordinated expression of functionally related proteins and can provide a mechanism for feedback regulation in metabolic pathways.

2. Sequestration of RBS

Ribosome binding site (RBS) sequestration is a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism where the accessibility of the RBS is controlled by RNA secondary structures or RNA-binding proteins.

In this mechanism, the RBS is hidden within a stable stem-loop structure, preventing ribosome binding and translation initiation. Regulatory signals, such as temperature changes, metabolite concentrations, or specific proteins, can alter this secondary structure to either expose or further hide the RBS.

Example:

The E. coli rpoH gene, which encodes the heat shock sigma factor σ32, uses temperature-sensitive mRNA secondary structures. At normal temperatures, the RBS is sequestered, but at high temperatures, the structure melts, exposing the RBS and allowing translation.

This mechanism allows for rapid responses to environmental changes without the need for new transcription, providing an efficient way to regulate protein production.

3. Regulation by UTR Elements

Untranslated regions (UTRs) at the 5' and 3' ends of mRNAs contain regulatory elements that control translation efficiency, mRNA stability, and localization.

5' UTR Regulation - Ferritin mRNA

The 5' UTR of ferritin mRNA contains an iron-responsive element (IRE) that forms a stem-loop structure. Under low iron conditions, iron regulatory proteins (IRPs) bind to the IRE, blocking the scanning of the 40S ribosomal subunit and inhibiting translation initiation.

Mechanism:

When cellular iron levels increase, IRPs dissociate from the IRE, allowing translation to proceed and ferritin production to increase, enabling iron storage.

3' UTR Regulation - Transferrin Receptor mRNA

The 3' UTR of transferrin receptor mRNA also contains IREs, but in this case, IRP binding under low iron conditions stabilizes the mRNA against degradation, increasing transferrin receptor production and iron uptake.

Mechanism:

When iron levels are high, IRPs dissociate, making the mRNA susceptible to endonucleolytic cleavage, reducing transferrin receptor production.

These coordinated regulatory mechanisms allow cells to maintain iron homeostasis through post-transcriptional control.

4. Global Down Regulation of Translation

Cells can implement global down-regulation of translation in response to various stresses, such as nutrient deprivation, heat shock, viral infection, or oxidative stress. This allows the cell to conserve energy and redirect resources to stress response pathways.

Key mechanisms for global translation regulation include:

Phosphorylation of eIF2α

Under stress conditions, various kinases phosphorylate the alpha subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2). Phosphorylated eIF2 acts as a competitive inhibitor of eIF2B, reducing the recycling of eIF2-GDP to eIF2-GTP, which is essential for translation initiation.

mTOR Pathway Regulation

The mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) pathway is a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism that integrates multiple signals including nutrient availability, energy status, growth factors, and cellular stress.

mTORC1 Regulation of Translation:

mTORC1 controls translation through two primary effectors:

4E-BP (eIF4E-Binding Protein)

• When dephosphorylated, 4E-BP binds to eIF4E and prevents its interaction with eIF4G

• mTORC1-mediated phosphorylation releases 4E-BP from eIF4E

• Allows formation of the eIF4F complex essential for cap-dependent translation

S6K (S6 Kinase)

• mTORC1 phosphorylates and activates S6K

• Active S6K phosphorylates multiple translation regulators including:

- eIF4B: Enhances helicase activity of eIF4A

- PDCD4: Targets it for degradation, relieving inhibition of eIF4A

Upstream Regulation of mTORC1:

mTORC1 activity is controlled by multiple upstream signals:

Nutrient Sensing

• Amino acids activate mTORC1 through Rag GTPases and Ragulator complex

• Glucose availability influences mTORC1 via AMPK signaling

Growth Factor Signaling

• PI3K/Akt pathway relieves inhibition allowing Rheb-GTP to activate mTORC1

• MAPK signaling can also activate mTORC1.

Energy Status

• AMPK under low energy conditions, inhibits mTORC1

• AMPK also phosphorylates Raptor, further inhibiting mTORC1

Physiological Significance:

During amino acid starvation, mTORC1 is rapidly inactivated, leading to:

• Hypophosphorylation of 4E-BP, inhibiting cap-dependent translation

• Reduced S6K activity, decreasing translation initiation

• Activation of autophagy to replenish amino acid pools

• Selective translation of stress-responsive mRNAs (e.g., via IRES elements)

These global regulatory mechanisms ensure cellular survival during unfavorable conditions by prioritizing essential functions.

5. Regulation by Short Upstream ORFs

Short upstream open reading frames (uORFs) are regulatory elements found in the 5' UTR of many eukaryotic mRNAs. They can modulate translation of the main downstream ORF in response to various cellular conditions.

When a ribosome translates a uORF, it may either dissociate from the mRNA or resume scanning and reinitiate at the downstream start codon. The efficiency of reinitiation depends on various factors, including the length of the uORF and the presence of regulatory elements.

Example: GCN4 mRNA Regulation

The yeast GCN4 mRNA contains four uORFs in its 5' UTR. Under non-starvation conditions, ribosomes typically translate uORF1 and then reinitiate at uORF2-4, preventing GCN4 translation.

During amino acid starvation, phosphorylation of eIF2α reduces translation initiation efficiency. This allows ribosomes to scan past uORF2-4 after translating uORF1 and reinitiate at the GCN4 start codon, increasing production of this transcriptional activator of amino acid biosynthesis genes.

uORF-mediated regulation provides a sophisticated mechanism for conditional control of gene expression at the translational level.

6. Regulation by IRES Elements

Internal ribosome entry site (IRES) elements are RNA structures that allow cap-independent translation initiation by recruiting ribosomes directly to an internal site on the mRNA, bypassing the need for the 5' cap structure.

IRES elements were first discovered in picornavirus RNAs but have since been found in cellular mRNAs, particularly those encoding proteins involved in stress response, apoptosis, and growth control.

Key Features:

• Enable translation when cap-dependent initiation is compromised (e.g., during stress, mitosis, or viral infection)

• Often require specific IRES trans-acting factors (ITAFs) for optimal function

• Can allow continued expression of essential proteins under conditions that inhibit global translation

Examples of cellular mRNAs containing IRES elements include those encoding c-Myc, Apaf-1, VEGF, and BIP. During apoptosis or endoplasmic reticulum stress, when cap-dependent translation is inhibited, these mRNAs continue to be translated via their IRES elements.

The presence of IRES elements provides an important mechanism for maintaining expression of critical regulatory proteins under adverse conditions.

7. Programmed Ribosome Frameshifting

Programmed ribosomal frameshifting is a translational recoding mechanism where the ribosome shifts reading frame at a specific mRNA location, allowing production of multiple proteins from a single mRNA.

This process typically occurs at "slippery" sequences followed by a secondary structure element (such as a pseudoknot) that causes the ribosome to pause. During this pause, tRNA-mRNA pairing can slip, resulting in a -1 or +1 frameshift.

Viral Examples:

Many viruses use programmed frameshifting to regulate the stoichiometry of their proteins. For example:

• HIV uses -1 frameshifting to produce the Gag-Pol polyprotein at a ratio of approximately 20:1 relative to Gag alone

• Coronavirus replication requires -1 frameshifting to produce the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase

Cellular examples also exist, such as the decoding of the antizyme mRNA, where +1 frameshifting is regulated by polyamine levels to control ornithine decarboxylase degradation.

Programmed frameshifting represents an elegant mechanism for expanding the coding capacity of genomes and regulating protein expression ratios without requiring additional transcriptional regulation.

8. Riboswitches

Riboswitches are regulatory elements in mRNA that directly bind small molecules (metabolites) to control gene expression without the need for protein factors. They are typically found in the 5' untranslated regions (UTRs) of bacterial mRNAs and some eukaryotic mRNAs.

Riboswitches consist of two main domains:

Riboswitch Structure:

Aptamer Domain

• Binds directly to a specific metabolite with high specificity and affinity

• Undergoes conformational change upon ligand binding

Expression Platform

• Transduces the binding signal into a regulatory response

• Can control transcription termination, translation initiation, or mRNA stability

Common Riboswitch Examples:

TPP (Thiamine Pyrophosphate) Riboswitch: Found in genes involved in thiamine biosynthesis. When TPP is abundant, it binds the riboswitch, promoting formation of a terminator structure that halts transcription.

Lysine Riboswitch: Regulates lysine biosynthesis genes. Lysine binding induces structural changes that sequester the ribosome binding site, preventing translation initiation.

FMN (Flavin Mononucleotide) Riboswitch: Controls riboflavin biosynthesis. FMN binding promotes formation of a transcription terminator or blocks ribosome access.

Mechanisms of Regulation

Riboswitches can regulate gene expression through several mechanisms:

Transcription Termination

• Ligand binding promotes formation of a terminator hairpin

• Causes RNA polymerase to dissociate prematurely

• Common in bacteria for metabolic pathway regulation

Translation Inhibition

• Ligand binding sequesters the ribosome binding site (RBS)

• Prevents ribosome binding and translation initiation

• Provides rapid response to metabolite levels

mRNA Cleavage

• Some riboswitches regulate mRNA stability

• Ligand binding can promote or inhibit ribozyme activity

• Controls mRNA half-life in response to metabolite concentration

Physiological Significance:

Riboswitches provide efficient feedback regulation for metabolic pathways:

• Allow direct sensing of metabolite concentrations without protein intermediaries

• Enable rapid response to changing cellular conditions

• Conserve energy by preventing unnecessary gene expression

• Found in essential pathways including vitamin biosynthesis, purine metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis
