Termination of Replication in Prokaryotes

1. The 'Replication Fork Trap'

In prokaryotes like E. coli, with their circular chromosomes, replication starts at a single origin (oriC) and proceeds bidirectionally. Two replication forks move in opposite directions around the circle.

Termination must be a precisely controlled event. It doesn't just happen randomly when the forks collide. Instead, the cell sets up a "replication fork trap" in the terminus region, which is located on the chromosome roughly opposite oriC.

This trap ensures that the forks meet within a specific, defined zone, preventing over-replication of the chromosome and coordinating the process with cell division.

2. Ter Sites: The 'Stop Signs'

The replication trap is formed by a series of short DNA sequences called Ter (termination) sites. These are ~23 base pair sequences.

In E. coli, there are multiple Ter sites (e.g., TerA, TerB, TerC, TerD, TerE) clustered in the terminus region. These sites are directional and organized into two opposing groups.

  • One group (e.g., TerA, TerD) blocks the counter-clockwise moving fork.
  • The other group (e.g., TerB, TerC) blocks the clockwise moving fork.

This arrangement creates a "trap" spanning several kilobases. A fork can enter the trap by passing Ter sites in its "permissive" direction but will be stopped when it encounters a Ter site in the "non-permissive" (blocking) direction.

3. Tus Protein: The 'Gatekeeper'

The Ter sites themselves are just binding platforms. The actual work of stopping the replication fork is done by the Tus (Terminus Utilization Substance) protein.

Tus is a monomeric protein that binds with extremely high affinity to a Ter site, forming a Tus-Ter complex. This complex is one of the most stable protein-DNA complexes known.

Crucially, the Tus-Ter complex is a polar (one-way) barrier. It has two distinct "faces":

  • Permissive Face: Allows the replication fork (specifically the DnaB helicase) to pass, displacing the Tus protein.
  • Non-Permissive Face: Physically blocks the DnaB helicase, arresting replication fork progression.

4. Mechanism: How Tus Blocks DnaB

The mechanism of the polar block is a fascinating example of protein-DNA interaction. The key is how the DnaB helicase, which unwinds the DNA, interacts with the Tus-Ter complex.

When DnaB approaches from the non-permissive side, it begins to unwind the Ter sequence. This unwinding exposes a specific, conserved cytosine base (C6) within the Ter sequence.

This exposed C6 base flips into a deep binding pocket on the Tus protein. This interaction induces a conformational change that "locks" the Tus protein onto the DNA with even greater force, creating an impassable roadblock for the helicase. Tus effectively acts as a "contra-helicase," preventing further unwinding.

When DnaB approaches from the permissive side, it unwinds the other strand of the Ter site. This does not trigger the C6 base-flipping mechanism. Instead, the helicase can displace the Tus protein from the DNA and continue on its way.

5. Post-Termination: Resolving Catenanes

Once both forks are stalled within the terminus region, replication is complete. However, this leaves a new problem: the two newly synthesized circular daughter chromosomes are interlocked, like links in a chain. This state is called a catenane.

These interlocked rings cannot be segregated into the daughter cells. The cell must resolve them using specialized enzymes called Type II Topoisomerases, specifically Topoisomerase IV (Topo IV) in E. coli.

Topo IV works by:

  1. Binding to one DNA duplex.
  2. Making a transient double-strand break in it.
  3. Passing the other intact DNA duplex through the break.
  4. Resealing the break.

This "decatenation" process separates the two chromosomes, allowing them to be properly segregated during cell division. This process is often aided by the FtsK translocase, which helps position the DNA at the division septum.
