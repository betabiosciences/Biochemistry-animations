Types of Mutations

DNA Damage, Lesions, and Mutations

DNA Damage: Physical or chemical alterations to the DNA molecule that may disrupt its normal structure or function. These can be caused by environmental factors like UV radiation, chemicals, or errors during DNA replication.

DNA Lesion: A specific site of damage in the DNA molecule. Lesions can include breaks in the DNA backbone, chemical modifications to bases, or cross-links between DNA strands.

DNA Mutation: A permanent alteration in the DNA sequence that makes up a gene. Mutations can range in size from a single DNA base pair to a large segment of a chromosome. Not all DNA damage leads to mutations if properly repaired.

Key Differences

DNA damage refers to abnormal chemical structures, while mutations are changes in the DNA sequence. Lesions are specific sites of damage that may or may not lead to mutations depending on DNA repair mechanisms.

Base Substitution Mutations

Base substitution mutations involve the replacement of one nucleotide base with another in the DNA sequence.

Transition

A purine base (Adenine or Guanine) is replaced by another purine, or a pyrimidine base (Cytosine or Thymine) is replaced by another pyrimidine.

Example: A → G or C → T

Transversion

A purine base is replaced by a pyrimidine base, or vice versa.

Example: A → C or G → T

Impact

Transitions occur more frequently than transversions because DNA polymerases are more likely to mispair similar-shaped bases.

Insertion/Deletion and Frameshift Mutations

Insertion/Deletion Mutations

These mutations involve the addition (insertion) or removal (deletion) of one or more nucleotide base pairs from the DNA sequence.

Frameshift Mutations

When insertions or deletions involve a number of base pairs that is not a multiple of three, they disrupt the reading frame of the gene. This alters all subsequent codons, typically resulting in a completely different amino acid sequence or premature stop codon.

In-Frame Mutations

When insertions or deletions involve a number of base pairs that is a multiple of three, the reading frame remains intact. These mutations add or remove entire amino acids from the protein without disrupting the rest of the sequence.

Note: Frameshift mutations generally have more severe consequences than in-frame mutations.

Effects of Point Mutations

Missense Mutation

A single nucleotide change that results in the substitution of one amino acid for another in the protein product.

Example: Sickle cell anemia results from a missense mutation that changes glutamic acid to valine in hemoglobin.

Nonsense Mutation

A point mutation that changes a codon for an amino acid into a stop codon, resulting in premature termination of protein synthesis.

Silent Mutation

A nucleotide substitution that does not change the amino acid sequence of the protein due to the degeneracy of the genetic code.

Neutral Mutation

A mutation that changes the amino acid sequence but does not significantly alter the protein's function, typically because the new amino acid has similar properties.

Additional Mutation Types

Forward Mutation

A mutation that changes a wild-type allele to a different allele.

Reverse Mutation

A mutation that changes a mutant allele back to the wild-type or a less severe mutant form.

Loss-of-Function Mutation

A mutation that reduces or eliminates the function of a gene product. These are typically recessive. It can be Amorphic or Hypomorphic. Amorphic mutations lead to complete absence of function, while hypomorphic mutations only partially reduce it.

Gain-of-Function Mutation

A mutation that confers new or enhanced activity to a gene product. It can be Hypermorphic or Neomorphic: A mutation that increases the normal function of the gene product (hypermorphic) or causes the gene product to exhibit a new function not seen in the wild type (neomorphic). These are often dominant.

Lethal Mutation

A mutation that results in the death of the organism, either during development or before reproductive age.

Suppressor Mutation

A mutation that counteracts the effects of another mutation, either within the same gene (intragenic) or in a different gene (extragenic).
