Prokaryotic Signaling & Chemotaxis

An Interactive Guide to Bacterial Sensing and Movement

General Features of Prokaryotic Signaling Pathways

Prokaryotic cells have evolved sophisticated signaling mechanisms to sense and respond to environmental changes. These pathways allow bacteria to detect nutrients, toxins, pH changes, temperature variations, and other stimuli crucial for survival.

Key Characteristics:

Speed: Prokaryotic signaling systems respond rapidly to environmental changes, often within seconds to minutes.

Simplicity: These systems typically involve fewer components compared to eukaryotic pathways, making them highly efficient.

Specificity: Each pathway is designed to respond to specific stimuli with appropriate cellular responses.

Two-Component System (TCS)

The most common signaling mechanism in prokaryotes is the two-component system, consisting of:

Sensor Histidine Kinase: Detects environmental signals and undergoes autophosphorylation
Response Regulator: Receives phosphate and mediates the cellular response

Mechanism of Two-Component Systems

When a stimulus is detected, the histidine kinase autophosphorylates at a conserved histidine residue using ATP. The phosphate group is then transferred to an aspartate residue on the response regulator, which becomes activated and triggers the appropriate cellular response, such as gene expression changes or enzymatic activity modulation.

Introduction to Chemotaxis

Definition

Chemotaxis is the directed movement of bacteria in response to chemical gradients in their environment. Bacteria can move toward favorable chemicals (attractants) or away from harmful ones (repellents).

Attractants and Repellents

Attractants include nutrients like sugars (glucose, galactose), amino acids (serine, aspartate), and oxygen. These chemicals signal favorable conditions for growth and survival.

Repellents include toxic substances, acids, heavy metals, and other harmful chemicals that bacteria must avoid to survive.

Flagellar Rotation and Swimming Behavior

Bacterial movement is controlled by the rotation direction of flagella:

Counterclockwise (CCW) Rotation: Default state leading to smooth swimming in straight lines
Clockwise (CW) Rotation: Causes tumbling motion, allowing bacteria to reorient randomly

Swimming Patterns

Smooth Swimming: When flagella rotate counterclockwise, they form a bundle that propels the bacterium forward in a relatively straight line.

Tumbling: Clockwise rotation disrupts the flagellar bundle, causing the bacterium to tumble and change direction randomly.

The default flagellar rotation is counterclockwise, resulting in smooth swimming. Bacteria modulate the frequency of tumbling to navigate chemical gradients effectively - reducing tumbling when moving toward attractants and increasing tumbling when moving toward repellents.

Structure of Bacterial Flagella

The bacterial flagellum is a complex molecular machine composed of over 20 different proteins. It consists of three main parts: the basal body, hook, and filament.

Major Structural Components

Basal Body: The motor embedded in the cell envelope
  • FliF: MS ring (membrane-supramembrane)
  • FliG, FliM, FliN: C ring (cytoplasmic)
  • FlgH, FlgI: P ring (peptidoglycan)
  • FlgA: L ring (lipopolysaccharide)
Hook (FlgE): Flexible coupling connecting basal body to filament
  • Allows torque transmission
  • Universal joint function
Filament (FliC): Long helical propeller made of flagellin subunits

Key Functional Proteins

MotA and MotB: Form the stator complex that generates torque
FliG: Rotor protein that interacts with MotA for rotation
FliM and FliN: Switch proteins that control rotation direction
CheY: Chemotaxis protein that binds to FliM to induce clockwise rotation

Assembly Process

Flagella are assembled from the inside out, starting with the basal body, then the hook, and finally the filament. The process is highly regulated and requires a type III secretion system to transport components through the growing structure.

Mechanism of Flagellar Rotation

The Flagellar Motor

The bacterial flagellar motor is a rotary engine that converts electrochemical energy into mechanical rotation. It's one of the most efficient motors known in biology.

Energy Source: Proton-Motive Force

The flagellar motor is powered by the proton-motive force (PMF) across the cell membrane. This electrochemical gradient consists of:

Chemical Component (ΔpH): pH difference across the membrane
Electrical Component (Δψ): Voltage difference across the membrane

Rotation Mechanism

The MotA/MotB stator complex forms proton channels that allow protons to flow down the electrochemical gradient. This proton flow drives conformational changes in the stator, which interact with the rotor protein FliG to generate torque.

Rotation Control

Default State: Counterclockwise rotation occurs when CheY is not phosphorylated

Switching State: Phosphorylated CheY-P binds to FliM, causing conformational changes that reverse rotation to clockwise

The motor can rotate at speeds up to 1,700 revolutions per second and can instantly reverse direction. Multiple MotA/MotB units (typically 8-11) surround the rotor to generate sufficient torque for swimming.

In some bacteria, particularly alkaliphilic species, the motor can also be driven by sodium ion gradients instead of proton gradients, demonstrating the versatility of this system.

Chemotaxis Signaling Pathway

Two-Component System in Chemotaxis

Chemotaxis employs a sophisticated two-component system with additional regulatory proteins:

Chemoreceptors (MCPs): Detect chemical stimuli (e.g., Tar, Tsr, Trg, Tap)
CheA: Histidine kinase that autophosphorylates
CheY: Response regulator controlling flagellar rotation
CheW: Coupling protein linking receptors to CheA
CheR: A methyltransferase that adds methyl groups to MCPs, contributing to adaptation.

Rotation in Clear Solutions

In the absence of chemical gradients (clear solutions), bacteria exhibit a random walk pattern:

• ~85% of time: Smooth swimming (CCW rotation)

• ~15% of time: Tumbling (CW rotation)

This results in no net directional movement

CheA is active, phosphorylating itself and then CheY. CheY-P binds to the flagellar motor, causing CW rotation (tumbling). CheZ rapidly dephosphorylates CheY-P, allowing a return to CCW rotation. This results in a balanced "run-and-tumble" motion.

Signaling in Presence of Attractants

Attractant Binding:

1. Attractant binds to chemoreceptor

2. Receptor undergoes conformational change

3. CheA kinase activity decreases

4. Less CheY gets phosphorylated

5. Lower CheY-P levels result in more CCW rotation

6. Tumbling frequency decreases → smooth swimming increases

Signaling in Presence of Repellents

Repellent Binding:

1. Repellent binds to chemoreceptor

2. CheA kinase activity increases

3. More CheY gets phosphorylated

4. Higher CheY-P levels cause more CW rotation

5. Tumbling frequency increases → bacteria reorient away

Adaptation Mechanism

The system adapts through methylation/demethylation of receptors by CheR (methyltransferase) and CheB (methylesterase), allowing bacteria to detect changes rather than absolute concentrations.

Chemotaxis Mutants

Studying chemotaxis mutants has provided crucial insights into the molecular mechanisms of bacterial motility and sensing. Three major classes of mutants are commonly studied:

fla- Mutants (Flagella-deficient)

Phenotype: Non-motile bacteria that lack flagella or have severely defective flagellar structures.

Affected Genes: Mutations in flagellar structural genes (fliC, flgE, flgG) or assembly genes (fliF, flgB).

Characteristics:

• Cannot swim in any direction

• May still show chemotaxis responses in swarm plate assays if partial flagellar function remains

• Used to study flagellar assembly and structure

mot- Mutants (Motility-deficient)

Phenotype: Have flagella but cannot rotate them, resulting in non-motile bacteria with intact flagellar structures.

Affected Genes: Mutations in motor genes (motA, motB) or switch genes (fliG, fliM, fliN).

Characteristics:

• Flagella are present but paralyzed

• Normal chemotaxis signaling but no physical movement

• Can be rescued by providing functional motor proteins

• Useful for studying motor function independently of flagellar assembly

che- Mutants (Chemotaxis-deficient)

Phenotype: Motile bacteria that cannot perform chemotaxis - they swim but show no directional bias toward attractants or away from repellents.

Affected Genes: Mutations in chemotaxis signaling genes (cheA, cheY, cheW, cheR, cheB) or receptor genes (tar, tsr, trg, tap).

Characteristics:

• Normal swimming ability but random movement patterns

• Cannot respond to chemical gradients

• May show constant tumbling (cheY overexpression) or smooth swimming (cheY deletion)

• Essential for understanding signal transduction mechanisms

Experimental Applications

These mutants are invaluable for:

• Complementation studies to identify gene functions

• Biochemical analysis of individual pathway components

• Understanding structure-function relationships

• Developing models of bacterial behavior and population dynamics
