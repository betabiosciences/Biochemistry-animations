Ionization State Details

1

Fully Protonated (Cationic) State

At low pH (below the pKa of the carboxyl group), both the carboxyl group (-COOH) and the amino group (-NH₃⁺) are protonated.

The amino acid carries a +1 net charge in this state.

Carboxyl Group

Protonated: -COOH

pKa ≈ 2.0-2.5

Charge: Neutral

Amino Group

Protonated: -NH₃⁺

pKa ≈ 9.0-10.0

Charge: Positive

2

Zwitterion (Neutral) State

At physiological pH (between pKa₁ and pKa₂), the carboxyl group is deprotonated (-COO⁻) and the amino group remains protonated (-NH₃⁺).

This dipolar form has a 0 net charge and is the predominant form at neutral pH.

Carboxyl Group

Deprotonated: -COO⁻

Charge: Negative

Amino Group

Protonated: -NH₃⁺

Charge: Positive

3

Fully Deprotonated (Anionic) State

At high pH (above the pKa of the amino group), both the carboxyl group (-COO⁻) and the amino group (-NH₂) are deprotonated.

The amino acid carries a -1 net charge in this state.

Carboxyl Group

Deprotonated: -COO⁻

Charge: Negative

Amino Group

Deprotonated: -NH₂

Charge: Neutral

About Amino Acid Electrophoresis

Amino acids can exist in different ionic states depending on the pH of the solution:

  • Cations (+): At pH below their isoelectric point (pI), amino acids carry a positive charge and move toward the negative electrode.
  • Anions (-): At pH above their pI, amino acids carry a negative charge and move toward the positive electrode.
  • Zwitterions (±): At pH equal to their pI, amino acids have both positive and negative charges that balance out, resulting in no net movement in an electric field. In this simulation, they migrate to the center.

The isoelectric point (pI) is the pH at which an amino acid carries no net electrical charge.

Amino Acid pI Values:

  • Glycine: 5.97
  • Lysine: 9.74
  • Aspartic Acid: 2.77
  • Histidine: 7.59

Suggested Experiments:

  1. Set pH to 7.00 and add all amino acids. Observe which ones move to which electrode.
  2. Set pH to 3.00 and observe how most amino acids become positively charged.
  3. Set pH to 11.00 and observe how most amino acids become negatively charged.
  4. Try to find the exact pH where a specific amino acid stops moving toward electrodes and starts moving to the center (its pI).
  5. Set pH to exactly 5.97 and add glycine to see it remain neutral at the center.
