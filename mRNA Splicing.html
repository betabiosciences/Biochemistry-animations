Eukaryotic mRNA Splicing

1. General Features of Splicing

Splicing is a critical step in eukaryotic gene expression that occurs in the nucleus. After a gene is transcribed into pre-mRNA (primary transcript), splicing removes non-coding regions called introns and joins the coding regions, or exons, together.

This precise cut-and-paste process ensures that only the protein-coding sequences are translated by the ribosome. It is performed by a massive molecular machine called the spliceosome. Splicing often occurs co-transcriptionally, meaning it happens while the pre-mRNA is still being synthesized by RNA polymerase II.

  • Product: Mature messenger RNA (mRNA).
  • Location: Nucleus.
  • Machinery: Spliceosome (for most introns).
  • Purpose: To create a continuous coding sequence for protein translation.

2. Features of Introns in Splicing

Spliceosomal introns have highly conserved sequences that act as signals for the splicing machinery. These cis-acting elements are recognized by components of the spliceosome.

  • 5' Splice Site (Donor Site): Located at the 5' end of the intron. It almost always has a GU dinucleotide sequence (the "GU-AG rule").
  • 3' Splice Site (Acceptor Site): Located at the 3' end of the intron. It is almost always marked by an AG dinucleotide.
  • Branch Point (or Branch Site): An adenine (A) residue located 15-45 nucleotides upstream of the 3' splice site. This adenine's 2'-OH group is crucial for the first catalytic step.
  • Polypyrimidine Tract (Py-tract): A stretch of 10-20 pyrimidines (cytosine and uracil) located between the branch point and the 3' splice site. It's essential for recruiting splicing factors to the 3' end of the intron.

3. The Splicing Mechanism (Two-Step Reaction)

The removal of an intron is a two-step chemical reaction, both of which are transesterification reactions. This means one phosphodiester bond is broken and another is formed simultaneously, requiring no net energy input (though ATP is used for spliceosome assembly).

  • Step 1: Lariat Formation

    The 2'-OH group of the branch point adenine (A) performs a nucleophilic attack on the phosphodiester bond at the 5' splice site. This breaks the bond between the 5' exon and the intron, and forms a unique 2'-5' phosphodiester bond. The intron curls into a loop structure called a lariat. The 5' exon is now free, possessing a 3'-OH group.

  • Step 2: Exon Ligation

    The newly freed 3'-OH group of the 5' exon performs a second nucleophilic attack, this time on the phosphodiester bond at the 3' splice site. This reaction joins the two exons together (ligates them) and releases the intron lariat, which is subsequently degraded in the nucleus.

4. The Spliceosome

The spliceosome is the massive, dynamic molecular complex that carries out pre-mRNA splicing. It is one of the largest complexes in the cell, comparable in size to a ribosome (approx. 4.8 MDa).

It is not a pre-formed machine; rather, it assembles step-wise onto the pre-mRNA at each intron. Its core components are five small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) and a large number of non-snRNP accessory proteins (over 150 different proteins).

The catalytic activity of the spliceosome is not performed by proteins, but by its RNA components, making it a ribozyme (an RNA enzyme).

5. Synthesis of snRNPs

The core of the spliceosome is built from small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, or snRNPs (pronounced "snurps"). Each snRNP consists of one small nuclear RNA (snRNA) molecule and a complex of associated proteins.

  • snRNAs: The five snRNAs involved in splicing are U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6. They are rich in uridine.
  • Transcription: U1, U2, U4, and U5 are transcribed by RNA Polymerase II (like mRNA). U6 is transcribed by RNA Polymerase III (like tRNA).
  • Assembly: The snRNAs (except U6) are exported to the cytoplasm. There, they associate with a ring of seven Sm proteins to form the snRNP core. After further modification (like hypermethylation of their 5' cap), they are re-imported into the nucleus to function. U6 remains in the nucleus, where it assembles with LSm (Sm-like) proteins.

6. Mechanism of Spliceosomal Splicing

Spliceosome assembly is a highly ordered, dynamic process involving multiple complexes:

  • E (Early) Complex: U1 snRNP binds to the 5' splice site via RNA-RNA base pairing. Splicing Factor 1 (SF1) binds the branch point.
  • A (Activation) Complex: U2 snRNP (aided by U2AF factor) binds to the branch point, displacing SF1. This binding forces the branch point adenine to bulge out, making its 2'-OH group available for catalysis.
  • B (Assembly) Complex: The pre-assembled U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP complex joins the A complex. At this stage, U4 and U6 are extensively base-paired, inactivating U6.
  • C (Catalytic) Complex: This is the activated spliceosome. Major RNA-RNA rearrangements occur:
    • U1 and U4 are released.
    • U6 displaces U1 at the 5' splice site.
    • U6 base-pairs with U2, forming the catalytic core.
    • U5 holds the 5' and 3' exons in close proximity.
  • Catalysis: The U2/U6 catalytic core facilitates the two transesterification reactions (lariat formation and exon ligation).
  • Disassembly: After exon ligation, the mature mRNA is released. The spliceosome disassembles, and the snRNPs are recycled for another round.

7. Regulation of Splicing

Splicing is not automatic; it is a major point of gene regulation. The choice of which splice sites to use is controlled by trans-acting protein factors that bind to cis-acting sequence elements on the pre-mRNA.

  • Splicing Enhancers (ESE/ISE): These are sequences within exons (Exonic Splicing Enhancers) or introns (Intronic Splicing Enhancers) that promote splicing at nearby sites.
  • SR Proteins: Enhancers are typically bound by SR proteins (rich in Serine and Arginine). SR proteins recruit U1 and U2AF to the correct splice sites, effectively "flagging" them for the spliceosome.
  • Splicing Silencers (ESS/ISS): These sequences (in exons or introns) repress splicing at nearby sites.
  • hnRNPs: Silencers are often bound by heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs). These proteins can block the binding of SR proteins or snRNPs, or cause the RNA to loop in a way that hides a splice site, leading to its exclusion.

The final splicing pattern is determined by the combinatorial code of these competing positive (SR) and negative (hnRNP) factors.

8. Alternative Splicing

Alternative splicing is a regulated process that allows a single gene to produce multiple distinct mRNA transcripts (isoforms) by selectively including or excluding certain exons.

This is a primary mechanism for generating proteomic diversity in eukaryotes. It is estimated that over 95% of human multi-exon genes undergo alternative splicing.

By changing the combination of exons, a cell can produce protein isoforms with different functions, localizations, or stabilities, all from the same gene. This is crucial for cell differentiation, development, and responding to environmental signals.

9. Types of Alternative Splicing

Alternative splicing can occur through several common patterns:

  • Constitutive Splicing: This is the "default" pattern where all introns are removed and all exons are joined in their linear order. It serves as the baseline.
  • Cassette Exon (Exon Skipping): The most common type. An exon (the "cassette") can be either included in the mature mRNA or skipped (spliced out along with its flanking introns).
  • Mutually Exclusive Exons: Only one exon from a cluster of two or more exons is included in the final mRNA, while all others are skipped.
  • Alternative Splice Sites (Cryptic Splicing): An alternative 5' or 3' splice site is used, making an exon longer or shorter. "Cryptic" sites are splicing signals that are normally hidden or weak but become activated under certain conditions.
  • Intron Retention: An entire intron is retained in the mature mRNA, rather than being spliced out. This often introduces a premature stop codon, leading to a truncated protein or degradation of the mRNA via Nonsense-Mediated Decay (NMD).

10. Self-Splicing Introns (Ribozymes)

Some introns can catalyze their own removal without the help of the spliceosome. These are known as self-splicing introns, or ribozymes. They are found in some bacteria, archaea, lower eukaryotes, and in the mitochondria and chloroplasts of plants.

  • Group I Introns: These introns fold into a complex 3D structure that binds an external guanosine (GTP, GDP, or GMP) as a cofactor. The 3'-OH of this G attacks the 5' splice site. The free 3'-OH of the 5' exon then attacks the 3' splice site, ligating the exons.
  • Group II Introns: These are mechanistically remarkable. They use an internal branch point adenine (A), just like the spliceosome. The 2'-OH of this A attacks the 5' splice site to form a lariat structure. This mechanism is so similar to spliceosomal splicing that Group II introns are believed to be the evolutionary ancestors of both nuclear introns and the snRNAs (like U2 and U6) that form the spliceosome's catalytic core.

11. Trans-Splicing

While standard (cis) splicing joins exons from the same pre-mRNA molecule, trans-splicing is a rare event that joins exons from two different pre-mRNA transcripts.

This process is common in protozoans (like trypanosomes) and lower invertebrates (like nematodes, e.g., *C. elegans*).

  • Mechanism: In these organisms, many genes are transcribed as long polycistronic (multi-gene) transcripts. Trans-splicing is used to cut this long transcript into individual, mature mRNAs.
  • Spliced Leader (SL) RNA: A short, common 5' exon, called the Spliced Leader (SL), is transcribed from its own gene. The SL RNA (in the form of an SL-snRNP) provides its exon to the 5' end of many different pre-mRNAs.
  • Function: This process simultaneously resolves the polycistronic message into monocistronic ones and adds a 5' cap (which is part of the SL RNA) to each mRNA, making it ready for translation.
