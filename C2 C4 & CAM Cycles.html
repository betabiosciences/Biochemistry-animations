About C2 Photosynthesis (Photorespiration)

1. Photorespiration Overview
  • Definition: Photorespiration is a metabolic pathway that consumes oxygen and releases carbon dioxide in the presence of light.
  • Trigger: Occurs when RuBisCO fixes oxygen instead of carbon dioxide.
  • Location: Takes place in three organelles: chloroplasts, peroxisomes, and mitochondria.
  • Energy Cost: Consumes ATP and reducing equivalents without producing sugars.
2. The C2 Cycle Process: Also known as the photorespiratory carbon oxidation cycle.
1. Oxygenation of RuBP: RuBisCO fixes O₂ instead of CO₂, producing phosphoglycolate and 3-phosphoglycerate.
2. Phosphoglycolate Processing: Phosphoglycolate is dephosphorylated to glycolate in chloroplasts.
3. Glycolate Transport: Glycolate moves to peroxisomes where it's oxidized to glyoxylate.
4. Glycine Formation: Glyoxylate is transaminated to glycine using glutamate.
5. Glycine to Serine: Glycine moves to mitochondria where two glycine molecules form one serine + CO₂ + NH₃.
6. Serine Processing: Serine returns to peroxisome and is converted to hydroxypyruvate then glycerate.
7. Glycerate to PGA: Glycerate moves back to chloroplast and is phosphorylated to 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA).
Biological Significance:
  • Carbon Salvage: Recovers 75% of carbon from phosphoglycolate that would otherwise be lost.
  • Nitrogen Metabolism: Involves amino acid metabolism (glycine, serine).
  • Stress Response: May play a role in plant stress responses and signaling.
  • Evolutionary Perspective: Represents an ancient metabolic pathway that became problematic when oxygen levels rose in Earth's atmosphere.
Environmental Factors:
  • Temperature: Photorespiration increases with temperature due to decreased CO₂ solubility and increased RuBisCO oxygenase activity.
  • Light Intensity: High light intensities can exacerbate photorespiration.
  • Water Stress: Stomatal closure under water stress limits CO₂ entry, favoring oxygenation.
  • CO₂:O₂ Ratio: Low CO₂:O₂ ratio increases photorespiration.

Photorespiratory Adaptations:
  • C4 Plants: Spatial separation of initial carbon fixation from RuBisCO activity.
  • CAM Plants: Temporal separation of carbon fixation (night) from Calvin cycle (day).
  • CO₂ Concentrating Mechanisms: Some algae and cyanobacteria use bicarbonate pumps.
  • RuBisCO Variants: Some organisms have RuBisCO with higher CO₂ specificity.
  • Engineering Approaches: Research focuses on introducing C4 traits into C3 crops to reduce photorespiratory losses.

About C4 Photosynthesis

1. The Evolutionary Problem: Photorespiration
  • RuBisCO's Dilemma: Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO), the key enzyme of carbon fixation in the Calvin cycle (C3 cycle), has dual activity. It catalyzes both:
    • Carboxylation: RuBP + CO₂ → 2 x 3PGA (Desired, productive)
    • Oxygenation: RuBP + O₂ → 3PGA + 2-phosphoglycolate (Wasteful & Toxic)
  • Photorespiration: The oxygenation reaction produces 2-phosphoglycolate, initiating the photorespiratory pathway. This process:
    • Consumes energy (ATP) and reducing power (NADPH).
    • Releases previously fixed CO₂.
    • Results in a significant net loss of carbon and energy (up to 30% in C3 plants under hot/dry conditions).
  • Environmental Trigger: Photorespiration increases dramatically with rising temperature and decreasing CO₂:O₂ ratio (common in hot, dry, bright environments where stomata close to conserve water, limiting CO₂ entry).
2. The C4 Solution: Spatial CO₂ Concentration: C4 photosynthesis is an elegant biochemical and anatomical adaptation that minimizes photorespiration by concentrating CO₂ around RuBisCO, effectively suppressing its oxygenase activity.
Initial CO₂ Fixation in MCs: CO₂ fixed into 4-carbon acid (OAA) using PEPC in mesophyll cytosol. PEPC has high affinity for CO₂ and no oxygenase activity.
Reaction: PEP + CO₂ + H₂O → OAA + Pi
Transport of C4 Acid: C4 acid (OAA/Malate/Aspartate) transported to Bundle Sheath Cells (BSCs) surrounding leaf veins.
Decarboxylation in BSCs: C4 acid decarboxylated in BSCs, releasing high concentration of CO₂.
Refixation by RuBisCO: Released CO₂ refixed by RuBisCO in BSC chloroplasts via Calvin cycle (C3 cycle), producing carbohydrates.
Regeneration of PEP: 3-carbon molecule (pyruvate/alanine) from decarboxylation transported back to MCs, converted to PEP using ATP via PPDK.
Reaction: Pyruvate + ATP + Pi → PEP + AMP + PPi (effectively 2 ATP equivalents)
Biochemical Advantages:
  • Suppressed Photorespiration: High [CO₂] in BSCs favors RuBisCO carboxylation.
  • Higher Water Use Efficiency (WUE): PEPC's high affinity allows efficient CO₂ fixation even when stomata are partially closed, reducing water loss per unit carbon fixed.
  • Higher Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE): Concentrating CO₂ allows BSC RuBisCO to operate near its maximum carboxylation rate. C4 plants often have less total RuBisCO protein (which is N-rich) than C3 plants for equivalent photosynthetic rates under favorable conditions.
  • Higher Photosynthetic Efficiency at High Temp/Light: C4 plants outperform C3 plants under high temperatures (30-45°C) and high light intensities due to minimized photorespiratory losses.
Energetic Cost: The C4 cycle requires 2 extra ATP per CO₂ fixed compared to the C3 cycle (to regenerate PEP via PPDK: Pyruvate + ATP → PEP + AMP + Pi → effectively 2 ATP equivalents). This cost is offset under conditions where photorespiration would otherwise consume large amounts of energy and carbon in C3 plants.

Animation Steps Flowchart

1. CO₂ Enters Leaf

Atmospheric CO₂ diffuses into the mesophyll cell through stomata.

2. Bicarbonate Conversion

CO₂ is converted to bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) in the mesophyll cell.

3. C4 Acid Formation

HCO₃⁻ combines with PEP to form a C4 acid.

4. Transport to Bundle Sheath

C4 acid diffuses into the bundle sheath cells via plasmodesmata.

5. Decarboxylation

C4 acid undergoes decarboxylation, releasing CO₂ and forming C3 acid.

6. Calvin Cycle & Sugar Production

Released CO₂ enters Calvin cycle for carbon fixation and sugar production.

7. PEP Regeneration

C3 acid returns to mesophyll cell and is converted back to PEP using ATP.

Anatomical Foundation: Kranz Anatomy:
  • Requires specific leaf anatomy: Kranz (German for "wreath") anatomy.
  • Vascular bundles are surrounded by one or more layers of large, chloroplast-rich Bundle Sheath Cells (BSCs).
  • Mesophyll Cells (MCs) surround the BSCs and are in direct contact with intercellular air spaces.
  • Key Feature: Extensive plasmodesmatal connections between MCs and BSCs facilitate rapid metabolite exchange.
  • Compartmentalization: The separation of initial fixation (MC) from decarboxylation and the Calvin cycle (BSC) is crucial for CO₂ concentration. BSCs often have reduced exposure to intercellular air spaces.

  • The primary C4 acid transported from MCs to BSCs.
  • The decarboxylation enzyme used in the BSCs.
  • The organelle where decarboxylation occurs.
  • The metabolite returned to MCs for PEP regeneration.

About CAM Photosynthesis

1. CAM Photosynthesis Overview
  • Definition: Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM) is a carbon fixation pathway that evolved in some plants as an adaptation to arid conditions.
  • Key Feature: Temporal separation of carbon fixation and the Calvin cycle.
  • Water Efficiency: CAM plants have extremely high water use efficiency.
  • Examples: Cacti, succulents, pineapple, orchids, and some ferns.
2. The CAM Cycle Process: Separation of carbon fixation into night and day phases.
Night Phase (Carbon Fixation): Stomata open at night to take in CO₂. CO₂ is fixed into organic acids (mainly malate) using PEP carboxylase and stored in vacuoles.
Day Phase (Calvin Cycle): Stomata close during the day to conserve water. Stored malate is decarboxylated, releasing CO₂ for the Calvin cycle.
Malate Storage: Malic acid accumulates in vacuoles during the night, causing cellular acidification.
Malate Release: During the day, malate is transported out of vacuoles and decarboxylated.
CO₂ Concentration: Released CO₂ is concentrated around RuBisCO, suppressing photorespiration.
PEP Regeneration: The 3-carbon product of decarboxylation is used to regenerate PEP for the next night cycle.
Ecological Advantages:
  • Water Conservation: Stomatal opening at night when evaporative demand is lower reduces water loss by up to 80% compared to C3 plants.
  • High Temperature Tolerance: CAM plants can photosynthesize efficiently at high temperatures when C3 plants would experience severe photorespiration.
  • Drought Resistance: Ability to close stomata completely during extended drought periods while maintaining basic metabolic functions.
  • Nutrient Use Efficiency: Often found in nutrient-poor environments where their conservative growth strategy is advantageous.
Types of CAM Plants:
  • Obligate CAM: Always perform CAM photosynthesis (e.g., most cacti).
  • Facultative CAM: Can switch between C3 and CAM depending on environmental conditions (e.g., some species of Mesembryanthemum).
  • CAM Cycling: Fix CO₂ at night but also fix some CO₂ during the day via C3 photosynthesis.
  • CAM Idling: Under extreme drought, stomata remain closed day and night, with CO₂ from respiration being refixed.

CAM Cycle Steps Flowchart

1. Night: CO₂ Uptake

Stomata open at night to take in CO₂ when evaporative demand is low.

2. Night: Carbon Fixation

CO₂ is fixed into malate using PEP carboxylase in cytosol.

3. Night: Malate Storage

Malate is transported into vacuoles and stored as malic acid.

4. Day: Stomata Close

Stomata close during the day to conserve water.

5. Day: Malate Release

Malate is transported out of vacuoles into cytosol.

6. Day: Decarboxylation

Malate is decarboxylated, releasing CO₂ and pyruvate.

7. Day: Calvin Cycle

Released CO₂ enters Calvin cycle for sugar production.

Anatomical and Biochemical Features:
  • Succulent Tissues: CAM plants typically have fleshy leaves or stems with large vacuoles for acid storage.
  • Reduced Stomatal Density: Fewer stomata than C3 or C4 plants, further reducing water loss.
  • Large Vacuoles: Specialized for storing malic acid overnight.
  • Enzyme Regulation: Key enzymes like PEP carboxylase are regulated by day/night cycles through phosphorylation/dephosphorylation.
  • pH Fluctuations: Dramatic daily pH changes in vacuoles (acidic at night, neutral during day).
