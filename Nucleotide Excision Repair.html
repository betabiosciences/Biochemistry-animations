Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER)

Overall Summary of NER

Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER) is a major DNA repair pathway that corrects "bulky" lesions in the DNA helix. These lesions are typically caused by environmental mutagens like ultraviolet (UV) light or chemical carcinogens. Examples include pyrimidine dimers (formed by UV radiation) and large chemical adducts that distort the DNA's double-helix structure.

The fundamental mechanism of NER can be described as a "cut and patch" process. It involves several key steps:

  • Damage Recognition: The cell's machinery constantly scans the DNA for structural distortions.
  • Incision/Excision: Once a bulky lesion is identified, the repair machinery makes two cuts in the damaged DNA strand—one on each side of the lesion. This results in the removal (excision) of a short, single-stranded DNA fragment containing the damage.
  • Synthesis: The resulting single-strand gap is filled in by a DNA polymerase, which uses the undamaged complementary strand as a template to synthesize a new, correct patch.
  • Ligation: Finally, a DNA ligase enzyme seals the nick, joining the newly synthesized patch to the rest of the DNA strand and fully restoring the helix.

This pathway is essential for maintaining genomic stability and preventing mutations that can lead to diseases, including cancer.

Prokaryotic NER Mechanism

In prokaryotes, such as *E. coli*, the NER pathway is primarily mediated by the Uvr (Ultraviolet repair) protein system, often called the UvrABC endonuclease.

Core Mechanism (Uvr Proteins)

  1. Damage Scanning & Recognition: A complex of two UvrA proteins and one UvrB protein (A2B) scans the DNA. UvrA is responsible for detecting helical distortions.
  2. Verification & Binding: Upon finding a potential lesion, the complex pauses. UvrB verifies the damage, and the complex's affinity for the DNA increases. UvrA then dissociates, leaving UvrB tightly bound to the damaged site.
  3. Incision (Excinuclease): UvrC is recruited to the UvrB-DNA complex. UvrC acts as the excinuclease, making two incisions: one 4-5 nucleotides 3' to the lesion and another 8 nucleotides 5' to the lesion. This brackets a 12-13 nucleotide fragment.
  4. Excision & Unwinding: UvrD (Helicase II) is recruited. It unwinds and removes the 12-13 nucleotide fragment containing the damage (along with UvrC). UvrB is also released.
  5. Synthesis & Ligation: DNA Polymerase I fills the gap, and DNA Ligase seals the final nick, completing the repair.

Sub-Pathways in Prokaryotes

  • Global Genomic Repair (GGR): This is the default pathway, where the UvrA2B complex scans the entire genome, including non-transcribed regions and the non-transcribed strand of genes, for damage.
  • Transcription-Coupled Repair (TCR): This pathway provides a rapid response to lesions that block transcription. When a stalled RNA polymerase (RNAP) encounters a lesion on the transcribed strand, it acts as a damage signal. The Mfd (Mutation Frequency Decline) protein recognizes the stalled RNAP, displaces it, and recruits the UvrA2B complex directly to the damage site, prioritizing the repair of active genes.

Eukaryotic NER Mechanism

Eukaryotic NER is significantly more complex, involving over 30 different proteins. However, it follows the same fundamental "cut and patch" logic and is also divided into two major sub-pathways based on how damage is initially recognized.

Sub-Pathways & Recognition

  • Global Genomic Repair (GGR): This pathway surveys the entire genome. Initial damage recognition is primarily performed by the XPC complex (which includes RAD23B and CETN2). This complex detects the helical distortion. For some types of damage (like certain UV lesions), the DDB1/DDB2 (XPE) complex may bind first and help recruit XPC.
  • Transcription-Coupled Repair (TCR): This pathway is initiated when a stalled RNA Polymerase II (RNAPII) encounters a lesion on the transcribed strand. The stalled polymerase is recognized by the CSB (Cockayne Syndrome B) protein, which then recruits the core NER machinery.

Core Repair Mechanism

After initial recognition by either GGR or TCR, the subsequent steps converge:

  1. Unwinding & Bubble Formation: The TFIIH (Transcription Factor II H) complex is recruited. TFIIH contains two helicases: XPB (unwinds 3' to 5') and XPD (unwinds 5' to 3'). These helicases unwind the DNA around the lesion, creating a stable "bubble" of about 30 nucleotides.
  2. Bubble Stabilization: XPA protein binds within the bubble to verify the damage and help position other proteins. RPA (Replication Protein A) binds to the undamaged single strand to protect it and stabilize the bubble.
  3. Dual Incision: Two endonucleases are recruited to cut the damaged strand. XPF (complexed with ERCC1) makes the 5' incision. XPG makes the 3' incision. These cuts excise a larger fragment than in prokaryotes, typically 24-32 nucleotides long.
  4. Synthesis & Ligation: The excised fragment is released. The gap is filled by DNA Polymerase δ (delta) or ε (epsilon), assisted by the sliding clamp PCNA. Finally, DNA Ligase I (or Ligase III) seals the nick.

Defects in these eukaryotic NER proteins (like XPA-XPG, or CSB) are linked to severe human genetic disorders, such as Xeroderma Pigmentosum (XP), which is characterized by extreme sun sensitivity and a high incidence of skin cancer, and Cockayne Syndrome (CS).
