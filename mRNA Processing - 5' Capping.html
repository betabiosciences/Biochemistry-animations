5' mRNA Capping in Eukaryotes

Structure of the 5' Cap

The 5' cap is a modified guanine nucleotide added to the 5' end of eukaryotic mRNA. This structure plays a crucial role in mRNA stability, transport, and translation initiation.

Core Structure

The basic 5' cap structure consists of:

  • A 7-methylguanosine (m7G) residue
  • Connected to the first transcribed nucleotide via a 5'-5' triphosphate bridge
  • The first nucleotide of the mRNA transcript may also be methylated

Chemical Composition

The 5' cap has the chemical formula: m7G(5')ppp(5')N, where:

  • m7G = 7-methylguanosine
  • ppp = triphosphate bridge
  • N = the first nucleotide of the mRNA

This unique 5'-5' linkage is distinct from the standard 3'-5' phosphodiester bonds found in the RNA chain, making the cap resistant to most 5' exonucleases.

Mechanism of Capping

The capping process occurs co-transcriptionally and involves three sequential enzymatic reactions that modify the 5' end of the nascent RNA transcript.

Step 1: Removal of Terminal Phosphate

The first step is catalyzed by RNA triphosphatase, which removes the γ-phosphate from the 5' triphosphate end of the nascent RNA, leaving a diphosphate terminus.

Step 2: GMP Addition

Guanylyltransferase then catalyzes the addition of a GMP moiety from GTP to the 5' diphosphate RNA, forming the G(5')ppp(5')N structure.

Step 3: Methylation

Finally, guanine-N7-methyltransferase transfers a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the N7 position of the terminal guanine, forming the mature m7G cap.

Additional Methylations

Depending on the organism and mRNA type, additional methylations may occur on the ribose of the first and second nucleotides, forming cap1 and cap2 structures respectively.

Different Types of Caps

While the basic m7G cap is common in yeast and lower eukaryotes, different organisms and contexts can produce variations in cap structure.

Cap 0

This is the basic cap structure found in lower eukaryotes: m7GpppN (where N is any nucleotide). It provides protection from exonucleases and facilitates translation initiation.

Cap 1

In this structure, the first transcribed nucleotide (N1) is methylated at the 2'-O position of the ribose: m7GpppN1m. This modification is common in higher eukaryotes and provides additional protection from degradation.

Cap 2

This structure has methylation at both the first and second nucleotides: m7GpppN1mpN2m. Cap2 is less common but provides enhanced stability and may play roles in specific regulatory contexts.

Cap 4 (Trimethyl Guanosine Cap)

This specialized cap structure, m2,2,7GpppN, features a trimethylated guanosine residue and is found primarily on:

  • Small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), especially those involved in splicing (U1, U2, U4, U5)
  • Some small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs)
  • Telomerase RNA component

The trimethyl guanosine cap (TMG cap) is formed by hypermethylation of the standard m7G cap, adding two additional methyl groups to the N2 position of guanine.

Significance of Capping mRNA

The 5' cap serves multiple essential functions in eukaryotic gene expression, affecting nearly every aspect of mRNA metabolism.

Protection from Degradation

The cap structure protects mRNA from 5'→3' exonucleases, significantly increasing mRNA half-life. Uncapped mRNAs are rapidly degraded in the cytoplasm.

Translation Initiation

The cap is recognized by the eIF4E complex, which recruits the 40S ribosomal subunit to initiate translation. This cap-dependent translation is the primary mechanism for protein synthesis in eukaryotes.

Nuclear Export

The cap-binding complex (CBC) associates with the 5' cap and facilitates mRNA export from the nucleus to the cytoplasm through nuclear pore complexes.

Splicing Regulation

The cap enhances the efficiency of pre-mRNA splicing, particularly for the first intron, by promoting the assembly of the spliceosome.

Immune Recognition

In vertebrates, the cap structure helps distinguish self-RNA from non-self RNA, as many viral RNAs lack proper 5' caps or have different cap structures.
