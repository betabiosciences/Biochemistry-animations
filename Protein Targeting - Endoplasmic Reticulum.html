Protein Targeting to Endoplasmic Reticulum

Protein Targeting: An Overview

Protein targeting, also known as protein sorting, is the mechanism by which cells transport proteins to their appropriate locations within or outside the cell. This process is crucial for maintaining cellular organization and function.

Key Features of Protein Targeting

  • Specificity: Proteins contain specific signal sequences that direct them to correct destinations
  • Energy Requirement: Most targeting processes require energy in the form of ATP or GTP
  • Receptor Recognition: Cellular receptors recognize and bind to signal sequences
  • Translocation: Proteins cross membrane barriers through specialized translocation channels (Translocons)
  • Folding Assistance: Molecular chaperones assist in protein folding during or after translocation

Types of Protein Targeting

Organellar Trafficking

Direct transport of proteins from cytosol to specific organelles such as:

  • Endoplasmic Reticulum
  • Mitochondria
  • Chloroplasts (in plants)
  • Peroxisomes
  • Nucleus

This process typically involves signal sequences and specific translocation machinery.

Vesicular Trafficking

Transport of proteins between membrane-bound compartments via vesicles:

  • Endoplasmic Reticulum → Golgi Apparatus
  • Golgi Apparatus → Plasma Membrane
  • Golgi Apparatus → Lysosomes
  • Endocytosis and Exocytosis

This pathway involves budding and fusion of transport vesicles with target membranes.

Mechanism of Organellar Trafficking

Organellar trafficking involves precise molecular mechanisms that ensure proteins reach their correct destinations within the cell.

Key Organelles Targeted

  • Endoplasmic Reticulum: Entry point for secretory pathway; proteins with ER signal sequences
  • Mitochondria: Proteins with mitochondrial targeting signals (MTS)
  • Chloroplasts: Proteins with chloroplast transit peptides
  • Peroxisomes: Proteins with peroxisomal targeting signals (PTS1 or PTS2)
  • Nucleus: Proteins with nuclear localization signals (NLS)

Co-translational Translocation

Proteins are translocated into the ER as they are being synthesized on ribosomes:

  • Signal Recognition Particle (SRP) binds to the signal sequence as it emerges from the ribosome
  • SRP-ribosome complex binds to SRP receptor on ER membrane
  • Ribosome transfers to the translocon (Sec61 complex)
  • Translation continues with polypeptide chain passing through translocon into ER lumen
  • Signal peptide is cleaved by signal peptidase
  • Common for secretory proteins and membrane proteins

Post-translational Translocation

Proteins are fully synthesized in cytosol before translocation:

  • Common in yeast ER
  • Mitochondria
  • Chloroplasts
  • Peroxisomes
  • Nucleus

Targeting to Endoplasmic Reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum serves as the entry point for the secretory pathway and is crucial for protein synthesis, folding, and modification.

Co-translational Translocation to ER

This is the primary pathway for protein import into the ER in mammalian cells:

  1. Signal Sequence Recognition: As the signal sequence emerges from the ribosome, it is recognized by the Signal Recognition Particle (SRP)
  2. Targeting to ER Membrane: SRP-ribosome complex binds to SRP receptor on ER membrane
  3. Translocon Engagement: Ribosome binds to Sec61 translocon complex
  4. Translocation: Polypeptide chain passes through Sec61 channel as translation continues
  5. Signal Cleavage: Signal peptidase cleaves the signal sequence
  6. Protein Folding: Chaperones (BiP, calnexin) assist in proper folding

Key Components

  • Signal Sequence: Located at N-terminus with a Hydrophobic core flanked by polar regions (usually 15-30 amino acids)
  • SRP (Signal Recognition Particle): Ribonucleoprotein that recognizes signal sequences
  • Sec61 Complex: Protein-conducting channel in ER membrane
  • Signal Peptidase: Enzyme that removes signal sequences
  • Oligosaccharyl Transferase: Enzyme complex that adds N-linked glycans

Post-translational Translocation in Yeast

Yeast cells extensively use post-translational translocation for ER import:

  • Proteins are fully synthesized in cytosol before translocation
  • Requires Sec62/Sec63 complex in addition to Sec61
  • Molecular chaperones (BiP) assist translocation
  • ATP hydrolysis by BiP provides energy for translocation
  • Sec72 and Sec71 proteins facilitate substrate recognition
  • Common for smaller proteins and under stress conditions

Targeting of Integral Membrane Proteins

Integral membrane proteins require specialized targeting mechanisms to ensure proper insertion and orientation in the ER membrane.

Topology Classification of Integral Membrane Proteins

Based on their orientation and number of transmembrane segments:

Type I Membrane Proteins

  • N-terminus in ER lumen, C-terminus in cytosol
  • Single transmembrane domain
  • Cleavable signal sequence at N-terminus
  • Example: LDL receptor

Type II Membrane Proteins

  • N-terminus in cytosol, C-terminus in ER lumen
  • Single transmembrane domain
  • Non-cleavable signal-anchor sequence
  • Example: Transferrin receptor

Type III Membrane Proteins

  • Similar to Type I but with non-cleavable signal sequence
  • N-terminus in ER lumen, C-terminus in cytosol
  • Example: Cytochrome P450

Type IV Membrane Proteins

  • Proteins with multiple transmembrane domains
  • Alternating orientation across the membrane
  • Example: G-protein coupled receptors

Targeting Mechanisms

Membrane protein targeting follows similar initial steps as soluble proteins but with additional complexity:

  • Signal-Anchor Sequences: Serve dual function as targeting signals and transmembrane anchors. These sequences contain a hydrophobic core that both targets the protein to the ER and remains embedded in the membrane as a transmembrane domain.
  • Stop-Transfer Sequences: Halt translocation and anchor protein in membrane. These hydrophobic sequences emerge from the translocon during protein synthesis and cause the translocon to open laterally, allowing the sequence to exit into the lipid bilayer.
  • Multipass Proteins: Use alternating signal-anchor and stop-transfer sequences. The first transmembrane segment typically functions as a signal-anchor sequence, while subsequent segments act as stop-transfer sequences, creating proteins with multiple membrane-spanning domains.
  • Positive-Inside Rule: Cytosolic domains tend to have more positively charged residues (lysine and arginine) flanking transmembrane segments. These charges influence orientation by interacting with the negatively charged phospholipid head groups on the cytosolic side of the membrane.

Tail-Anchored (TA) Proteins

Tail-anchored proteins represent a specialized class of membrane proteins with unique targeting mechanisms:

  • Structural Features: Consist of an N-terminal functional domain in the cytosol and a single C-terminal transmembrane domain that anchors the protein to membranes
  • Targeting Challenge: Their C-terminal transmembrane domain emerges from the ribosome only after translation is complete, making co-translational targeting impossible
  • Targeting Pathways:
    • GET Pathway (Guided Entry of Tail-anchored proteins): Primary pathway in yeast and mammals
      • SRP-independent targeting mechanism
      • Get3 ATPase acts as a targeting factor that binds to the transmembrane domain
      • Get1/Get2 complex serves as the ER membrane receptor
      • ATP hydrolysis drives membrane insertion
  • Examples: SNARE proteins (syntaxins, synaptobrevins), Bcl-2 family proteins, protein translocation components

GPI-Linked Proteins

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored proteins are membrane proteins attached to the lipid bilayer via a glycolipid anchor:

  • Biosynthesis:
    • Synthesized as single-pass transmembrane proteins with both N-terminal signal sequence and C-terminal hydrophobic sequence
    • After translocation into the ER lumen, the C-terminal sequence is cleaved and replaced with a pre-formed GPI anchor
    • This transamidation reaction is catalyzed by GPI transamidase complex
  • Structural Features:
    • Consist of a phosphoethanolamine linker, glycan core (mannose and glucosamine), and phosphatidylinositol lipid anchor
    • The complete GPI anchor is assembled on the luminal side of the ER membrane before attachment
  • Sorting Signals:
    • The C-terminal hydrophobic sequence serves as both a translocation signal and GPI attachment signal
    • A cleavage/attachment site (ω site) is located approximately 10-12 amino acids upstream of the C-terminus
  • Functional Significance:
    • Concentrated in lipid rafts due to saturated fatty acids in the GPI anchor
    • Can be selectively released from membranes by specific phospholipases
    • Important for cell signaling, adhesion, and immune recognition
  • Examples: Alkaline phosphatase, acetylcholinesterase, prion proteins, Thy-1, and many cell surface antigens
