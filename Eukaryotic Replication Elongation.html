🧬 DNA Replication Elongation in Eukaryotes

Introduction to Eukaryotic Replication

DNA replication in eukaryotes is a highly coordinated and complex process that ensures accurate duplication of the entire genome before cell division. The elongation phase represents the core synthetic stage where new DNA strands are continuously synthesized.

Key Distinction: Unlike prokaryotes, eukaryotic replication involves multiple origins of replication (10,000-100,000 origins in humans), shorter Okazaki fragments (~100-200 nucleotides), and more complex protein machinery.

Overview of the Process

Elongation occurs bidirectionally from each replication origin, creating replication forks that move in opposite directions. Each fork contains a sophisticated assembly of proteins called the replisome that coordinates the synthesis of both leading and lagging strands simultaneously.

The process must overcome several challenges including chromatin structure, topological stress, and maintaining fidelity while achieving speeds of approximately 50 nucleotides per second per fork.

The Replisome Complex

The replisome is the molecular machine responsible for DNA synthesis during elongation. It consists of numerous proteins that work in concert to replicate the genome with remarkable precision.

Core Components

  • DNA Polymerase δ (Pol δ): Primary polymerase for lagging strand synthesis; also completes leading strand synthesis
  • DNA Polymerase ε (Pol ε): Principal polymerase for leading strand synthesis
  • PCNA (Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen): Sliding clamp that tethers polymerases to DNA, increasing processivity dramatically
  • RFC (Replication Factor C): Clamp loader that loads PCNA onto DNA in an ATP-dependent manner
Processivity Enhancement: PCNA increases the processivity of DNA polymerases from ~20 nucleotides to over 5,000 nucleotides per binding event, making continuous synthesis possible.

Additional Regulatory Proteins

The replisome includes numerous accessory factors such as RPA (Replication Protein A) which coats single-stranded DNA, topoisomerases that relieve tension, and helicases that unwind the double helix.

Leading Strand Synthesis

The leading strand is synthesized continuously in the 5' to 3' direction, following the direction of replication fork movement. This strand is synthesized with relative simplicity compared to the lagging strand.

The Process

After Primase (Pol α-primase complex) synthesizes an initial RNA primer of approximately 8-12 nucleotides followed by 20-30 DNA nucleotides, DNA Polymerase ε takes over and extends the primer continuously.

High Fidelity: Pol ε has 3' to 5' exonuclease activity, allowing it to proofread and excise incorrectly incorporated nucleotides. This reduces error rate to approximately 1 in 10⁷ nucleotides.

PCNA's Role

The PCNA clamp encircles the DNA like a ring and slides along it, maintaining association with Pol ε. This processivity factor is essential for efficient continuous synthesis across long stretches of DNA without dissociation.

Lagging Strand Synthesis

The lagging strand presents a more complex challenge because DNA polymerases can only synthesize in the 5' to 3' direction, but the strand must be synthesized opposite to fork movement.

Okazaki Fragments

The solution is discontinuous synthesis through short segments called Okazaki fragments (100-200 nucleotides in eukaryotes). Each fragment requires its own priming event and subsequent processing.

Synthesis Cycle

  • Pol α-primase synthesizes a short RNA-DNA primer (~30-40 nucleotides total)
  • RFC recognizes the primer-template junction and loads PCNA
  • DNA Polymerase δ displaces Pol α and extends the fragment
  • Pol δ synthesizes until it encounters the previous Okazaki fragment
  • The cycle repeats every 100-200 nucleotides
Coordination: Despite the complexity, leading and lagging strand synthesis occur simultaneously with the replisome maintaining coordination between both processes through physical linkage of the polymerases.

Okazaki Fragment Processing

After Okazaki fragments are synthesized, they must be processed to create a continuous DNA strand. This involves removing RNA primers and ligating the fragments together.

Primer Removal

Two main pathways exist for removing RNA primers from Okazaki fragments:

Short Flap Pathway: Pol δ displaces a short flap from the primer region of the Okazaki fragment. FEN1 (Flap Endonuclease 1) then cleaves the cleaves the flap as Pol δ extends the upstream fragment, moving the nick further downstream."
Long Flap Pathway: Pol δ displaces a short flap from the primer region of the Okazaki fragment. It is further unwound by Pif1 displacing the downstream primer and creating a longer flap (>10 nucleotides). These are bound by RPA and then processed by Dna2 nuclease/helicase in conjunction with FEN1.

Ligation

After primer removal and gap filling by Pol δ, DNA Ligase I seals the nick between adjacent Okazaki fragments by catalyzing phosphodiester bond formation. This enzyme requires ATP and creates a continuous phosphate-sugar backbone.

DNA Ligase I specifically recognizes the PCNA clamp, ensuring it acts at appropriate sites along the lagging strand.

Managing Topological Stress

As the replication fork advances, unwinding the double helix creates severe topological problems. The rotation of DNA ahead of the fork generates positive supercoils that must be resolved to prevent fork stalling.

The Topological Problem

For every 10 base pairs unwound (one helical turn), one positive supercoil forms ahead of the replication fork. Without resolution, this overwinding would halt replication within seconds.

Topoisomerase Solutions

Topoisomerase I relieves positive supercoils by creating transient single-strand breaks, allowing rotation and relaxation before resealing. This enzyme works continuously ahead of each replication fork.

Topoisomerase II (Topo II) is essential for resolving more complex topological issues, particularly catenanes (interlinked DNA circles) that form when replication forks from adjacent origins converge. Topo II creates transient double-strand breaks and passes another DNA duplex through before resealing.

Clinical Relevance: Many anticancer drugs target topoisomerases, exploiting the fact that rapidly dividing cancer cells are particularly sensitive to replication stress. Examples include camptothecins (Topo I inhibitors) and etoposide (Topo II inhibitor).

Replication Through Chromatin

Unlike prokaryotic DNA, eukaryotic DNA is packaged into chromatin with histones forming nucleosomes. The replisome must navigate this complex structure during elongation.

Nucleosome Disruption

Ahead of the replication fork, nucleosomes must be disassembled or displaced to allow the replisome access to DNA. Histone chaperones like FACT (Facilitates Chromatin Transcription) and Asf1 facilitate this process by temporarily binding histones removed from DNA.
