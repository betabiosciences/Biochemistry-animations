DNA Double-Strand Break Repair

Section 1: The Double-Strand Break (DSB)

A Double-Strand Break (DSB) is the most cytotoxic form of DNA damage, where both strands of the DNA helix are severed. If left unrepaired, or repaired incorrectly, DSBs can lead to chromosomal rearrangements, genomic instability, and ultimately cell death (apoptosis) or carcinogenesis.

These breaks are generated by both endogenous sources (e.g., reactive oxygen species, replication fork collapse) and exogenous agents (e.g., ionizing radiation, chemotherapeutics).

To combat this threat, eukaryotic cells have evolved two primary, sophisticated pathways for DSB repair:

  • Homology Directed Repair (HDR): An "error-free" pathway that uses an undamaged homologous DNA sequence as a template.
  • Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ): A more "error-prone" pathway that directly ligates the broken ends together, often with small insertions or deletions.

Section 2: Homology Directed Repair (HDR)

HDR is a high-fidelity repair mechanism restricted to the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle, as it critically requires the presence of a sister chromatid to serve as a repair template.

Key Steps in HDR:

  1. Break Detection & Resection: The DSB is first recognized by the MRN complex (Mre11-Rad50-Nbs1), which, along with CtIP, initiates the 5' to 3' resection of the broken ends. This resection is extended by exonucleases like EXO1 and DNA2, creating long 3' single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) tails.
  2. RPA Coating: The newly formed ssDNA tails are immediately coated by Replication Protein A (RPA) to protect them from degradation and remove secondary structures.
  3. RAD51 Filament Formation: The tumor suppressor protein BRCA2 acts as a critical mediator, displacing RPA and loading the recombinase RAD51 onto the ssDNA tails. RAD51 forms a nucleoprotein filament, which is the active machinery for the homology search.
  4. Homology Search & Strand Invasion: The RAD51 filament searches the genome for a homologous sequence. Upon finding the sister chromatid, it invades the intact duplex, displacing one strand and forming a Displacement-loop (D-loop).
  5. DNA Synthesis & Resolution: A DNA polymerase extends the invading 3' end, using the intact strand as a template. This can be resolved via two main sub-pathways:
    • Synthesis-Dependent Strand Annealing (SDSA): The newly synthesized strand is displaced, unwinds from the D-loop, and anneals to the other resected end. Gaps are filled and ligated. This is the most common HDR outcome in mammals and does not result in crossover.
    • Double-Holliday Junction (dHJ) Pathway: The second broken end is captured, leading to the formation of two Holliday Junctions. These can be "dissolved" (by the BTR complex, no crossover) or "resolved" (by resolvases, can result in crossover or non-crossover).

Section 3: Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ)

NHEJ is the dominant DSB repair pathway in mammalian cells, active throughout all cell cycle phases (especially G1). It is much faster than HDR but is inherently mutagenic, as it directly ligates ends without a template, often causing small insertions or deletions (indels).

Key Steps in NHEJ:

  1. End Recognition: The Ku70/Ku80 heterodimer, a ring-shaped complex, rapidly binds to the broken DNA ends. It acts as a scaffold, protecting the ends from resection and recruiting other NHEJ factors.
  2. Synapsis & Kinase Activation: Ku recruits the catalytic subunit of the DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PKcs). DNA-PKcs bridges the two ends, forming a synaptic complex. Its kinase activity is activated, leading to autophosphorylation and the phosphorylation of other repair factors.
  3. End Processing: DSB ends are often "dirty" (not blunt 5'-P and 3'-OH). NHEJ employs a toolkit to clean them:
    • Artemis: An endonuclease (activated by DNA-PKcs) that trims 5' and 3' overhangs and opens hairpin structures.
    • PNKP (Polynucleotide Kinase Phosphatase): Corrects the end chemistry, adding 5' phosphates and removing 3' phosphates.
    • Polymerases μ and λ: Family X polymerases that fill in small gaps, often in a template-independent manner.
  4. Ligation: The final step is performed by the Ligase IV-XRCC4-XLF complex. This complex is recruited to the site and ligates the processed ends, sealing the break.

Section 4: Pathway Choice (HDR vs. NHEJ)

The decision between HDR and NHEJ is one of the most critical events in DSB repair. The choice is not random and is tightly regulated, primarily by the cell cycle.

Key Determinants:

  • Cell Cycle Phase: This is the master regulator.
    • G1 Phase: No sister chromatid is available, so HDR is impossible. The cell actively suppresses resection, and NHEJ is the only option.
    • S & G2 Phases: A sister chromatid is present, making HDR possible. The cell actively promotes HDR.
  • The Resection "Switch": The initiation of 5' end resection is the key commitment step.
    • In S/G2, CDKs (Cyclin-Dependent Kinases) are highly active. They phosphorylate CtIP, activating its resecting function with the MRN complex. This resection evicts the Ku proteins and commits the cell to HDR.
    • In G1, CDK activity is low. CtIP is not phosphorylated, resection is suppressed, and the Ku proteins bind stably, committing the cell to NHEJ.
  • Protein Competition: At the break site, there is a "race" between the Ku complex (for NHEJ) and the MRN/CtIP complex (for HDR). The cell cycle state tips the balance of this competition.
  • Chromatin Structure: The local chromatin environment (e.g., euchromatin vs. heterochromatin) also influences the accessibility of the break to repair factors and can bias the pathway choice.
