The SOS DNA Repair System

1. What is the SOS Response?

The SOS response is a global emergency response in prokaryotes, like E. coli, triggered by extensive DNA damage. When the cell's replication machinery encounters damage it can't fix, it stalls. This stalling exposes single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), which is the primary distress signal.

This isn't a single pathway, but rather a regulon—a collection of over 40 different genes—that is switched on to deal with the crisis. The system's goal is simple: survive at all costs, even if it means introducing mutations.

  • Repressor (Normal State): LexA protein keeps the SOS genes turned OFF.
  • Sensor (Damage State): RecA protein detects the ssDNA signal.

2. The Trigger: RecA Nucleofilament

The SOS response is initiated when RecA proteins encounter the ssDNA patches that form at stalled replication forks or DNA gaps.

RecA polymerizes onto this ssDNA, forming a helical RecA-ssDNA nucleoprotein filament. This structure is the activated form of RecA, often denoted as RecA*.

This RecA* filament is now an active co-protease, meaning it can help other proteins cut themselves. Its primary target is the LexA repressor.

3. LexA Autocatalytic Cleavage

In a healthy cell, the LexA repressor protein binds to specific 20-base-pair DNA sequences called SOS boxes. These boxes are located in the promoter region of all SOS genes, physically blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing them.

The activated RecA* filament finds and binds to LexA. This binding induces a conformational change in LexA, activating its *own* dormant protease function. LexA then cleaves itself in half (a process called autocatalysis).

The cleaved LexA fragments can no longer bind to the SOS boxes. They fall off the DNA, de-repressing the entire SOS regulon. The genes are rapidly transcribed and translated, flooding the cell with repair proteins.

4. Key Genes of the SOS Regulon

Once LexA is cleared, a diverse set of genes is expressed. They fall into several categories:

  • Error-Free Repair: Genes like uvrA and uvrB are upregulated, boosting Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER) to remove the damage.
  • Recombination: The recA gene itself is an SOS gene, creating a positive feedback loop. Other genes like recF, recO, and recR involved in recombinational repair are also induced.
  • Cell Division Block: The sulA (or sfiA) gene is expressed. SulA protein halts cell division. This buys the cell time to repair its chromosome before attempting to pass it to daughter cells.
  • Error-Prone Repair (The "Last Resort"): The umuD and umuC genes are expressed. These are the core of the mutagenic part of the SOS response.

5. Error-Prone Repair: Translesion Synthesis

This is the most "famous" and dangerous part of the SOS response. When the cell's main polymerase (Pol III) encounters a lesion (like a thymine dimer) that it cannot read, it stalls.

The umuD and umuC proteins are produced. The UmuD protein must also be cleaved by RecA* to become its active form, UmuD'.

Two UmuD' proteins and one UmuC protein assemble to form DNA Polymerase V (Pol V).

Pol V is a special "low-fidelity" polymerase. It has no proofreading (3'→5' exonuclease) activity and a loose, open active site. It temporarily displaces Pol III at the stalled fork, synthesizes a short patch of DNA *directly across* the lesion (this is Translesion Synthesis or TLS), and then falls off, allowing Pol III to resume.

Because Pol V cannot read the damaged template, it often inserts random bases. This bypasses the lesion, allowing replication to complete, but it comes at the cost of introducing mutations.

6. De-induction: Shutting Down the System

The SOS response is not meant to be active forever. As the various repair pathways (NER, recombination, TLS) fix the DNA damage, the amount of ssDNA in the cell decreases.

  • Without ssDNA, the RecA* filaments disassemble back into inactive RecA monomers.
  • Without RecA*, LexA is no longer being cleaved.
  • The cell is *always* slowly producing new, intact LexA (from a baseline-level-expressed lexA gene, which is also partially repressed by itself).
  • These new LexA proteins accumulate, bind to the SOS boxes, and one by one, shut off the SOS genes.
  • The unstable SulA protein is degraded, allowing cell division to resume.
  • The cell returns to its normal, non-SOS state.

7. Significance and Summary

The SOS response is a remarkable example of a cellular "life-or-death" decision. It is a high-risk, high-reward strategy.

By activating Translesion Synthesis, the cell accepts a high probability of mutagenesis in exchange for a chance at survival. A cell with a few mutations is better than a dead cell with an incomplete chromosome.

This mechanism is a major driver of bacterial evolution. It is also clinically important, as the mutagenic activity of the SOS response can accelerate the acquisition of antibiotic resistance. Some antibiotics, in fact, work by *causing* DNA damage, which ironically can trigger the very SOS response that allows the bacteria to mutate and survive the drug.
