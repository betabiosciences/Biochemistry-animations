Bacterial Photosynthesis

Exploring the diverse mechanisms of light energy conversion in bacteria

Oxygenic Photosynthesis in Cyanobacteria

Cyanobacteria are unique among bacteria for performing oxygenic photosynthesis, similar to plants and algae. This process uses water as an electron donor and produces oxygen as a byproduct.

Key Differences from Plant Photosynthesis

Feature Cyanobacteria Plants
Primary Pigments Chlorophyll a, Phycobilins Chlorophyll a, Chlorophyll b
Accessory Pigments Phycocyanin, Allophycocyanin, Phycoerythrin Carotenoids
Light-Harvesting Complex Phycobilisomes LHC I and LHC II
Location Thylakoid membranes (not in chloroplasts) Thylakoid membranes in chloroplasts

Phycobilisomes

These are large, water-soluble protein complexes attached to the thylakoid membranes. They contain phycobiliproteins that efficiently capture light energy and transfer it to chlorophyll a in the reaction centers.

  • Allow cyanobacteria to thrive in various light conditions
  • Enable absorption of light wavelengths that chlorophyll cannot efficiently absorb
  • Organized in rod-like structures with a core of allophycocyanin

Anoxygenic Photosynthesis in Purple Bacteria

Purple bacteria perform anoxygenic photosynthesis, which does not produce oxygen. They use reduced compounds like hydrogen sulfide, sulfur, or organic molecules as electron donors.

Key Characteristics

  • Use bacteriochlorophyll a or b as primary pigments
  • Have a single photosystem (PS I-like)
  • Perform cyclic electron flow to generate ATP
  • Can grow photoautotrophically or photoheterotrophically

Light Reaction Details

Cyclic Electron Flow Process

1
Light energy is absorbed by antenna complexes (LH1 and LH2)
2
Energy is transferred to the reaction center (P870 or P960)
3
Excited electron is transferred to a primary acceptor (bacteriopheophytin)
4
Electron moves through quinone pool (ubiquinone)
5
Cytochrome bc1 complex pumps protons across the membrane
6
Electron returns to the reaction center via cytochrome c2
7
Proton gradient drives ATP synthesis via ATP synthase

Photosynthetic Apparatus

The photosynthetic membranes of purple bacteria form extensive intracytoplasmic membranes that increase the surface area for light absorption.

  • Reaction centers are surrounded by light-harvesting complexes
  • Light-harvesting complexes contain carotenoids that absorb light and transfer energy to bacteriochlorophyll
  • Different types of light-harvesting complexes (LH1, LH2) work together to capture light efficiently

Unlike cyanobacteria and plants, purple bacteria do not have two separate photosystems and do not perform non-cyclic electron flow that would generate NADPH directly.

Anoxygenic Photosynthesis in Green Sulfur Bacteria

Green sulfur bacteria are obligate anaerobic phototrophs that perform anoxygenic photosynthesis using reduced sulfur compounds as electron donors.

Unique Features

  • Use bacteriochlorophyll c, d, or e as main pigments
  • Extremely efficient at harvesting light in low-light conditions
  • Have a non-cyclic electron flow that generates both ATP and NADPH
  • Can fix carbon dioxide via the reverse TCA cycle

Light Reaction Details

Non-Cyclic Electron Flow Process

1
Light energy is captured by chlorosomes and transferred to the reaction center (P840)
2
Excited electron is transferred through iron-sulfur centers
3
Electron reduces ferredoxin, which then reduces NAD+ to NADH
4
The electron "hole" in P840 is filled by electrons from reduced sulfur compounds (H₂S, S⁰)
5
Sulfur oxidation generates protons, contributing to the proton gradient
6
Proton gradient drives ATP synthesis via ATP synthase

The Chlorosome Structure

Green sulfur bacteria possess unique light-harvesting complexes called chlorosomes, which are the most efficient light-harvesting structures known in nature.

  • Chlorosomes are oval-shaped organelles attached to the cytoplasmic membrane
  • They contain thousands of bacteriochlorophyll molecules arranged in rod elements
  • This arrangement allows for efficient energy transfer through exciton coupling
  • Chlorosomes are surrounded by a lipid monolayer instead of a protein shell
  • They can constitute up to 30% of the cell's dry weight

The exceptional light-harvesting capability of chlorosomes enables green sulfur bacteria to thrive in extremely low-light environments, such as the deep layers of aquatic habitats where little light penetrates.

Comparing Bacterial Photosynthesis

Different bacteria have evolved diverse photosynthetic strategies to thrive in various ecological niches.

Evolutionary Significance

Cyanobacteria were responsible for the Great Oxygenation Event approximately 2.4 billion years ago, which dramatically changed Earth's atmosphere and paved the way for aerobic life.

Ecological Roles

  • Cyanobacteria: Major primary producers in aquatic ecosystems; some form harmful algal blooms
  • Purple Bacteria: Important in sulfur cycles; used in wastewater treatment
  • Green Sulfur Bacteria: Thrive in anoxic, sulfide-rich environments; contribute to carbon and sulfur cycling

Biotechnological Applications

Understanding bacterial photosynthesis has led to various applications:

  • Biofuel production using cyanobacteria
  • Development of artificial photosynthetic systems
  • Biosensors based on photosynthetic pigments
  • Wastewater treatment using photosynthetic bacteria

Research into bacterial photosynthesis continues to reveal new insights into energy conversion, with potential applications in sustainable energy technologies.
