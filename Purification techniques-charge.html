Protein Purification by Charge

Explore the principles behind charge-based separation techniques.

1. Ion Exchange Chromatography

This technique separates proteins based on their net charge at a specific pH. It uses a stationary phase with charged beads that bind to oppositely charged proteins. The separation is achieved by changing the buffer conditions, usually by increasing salt concentration or altering the pH, to elute the bound proteins.

Ion exchange chromatography separates proteins based on their net surface charge. The stationary phase contains charged functional groups that attract oppositely charged proteins. Proteins bind to the column resin through electrostatic interactions and are eluted by increasing the ionic strength of the buffer, which competes with proteins for binding sites.

Cation Exchange Chromatography 🧪

Separates positively charged proteins. The stationary phase has a negative charge. Positively charged proteins bind to the column, while others flow through. Cation exchange chromatography uses a negatively charged stationary phase to bind positively charged proteins (cations). The stationary phase typically contains functional groups like sulfonate (–SO3−) or carboxylate (–COO−). Proteins with net positive charge at the operating pH will bind to the column.

The diagram shows a cation exchange column. The negatively charged beads in the column (blue circles with a '-') attract and bind to the positively charged proteins (red circles with a '+'). The negatively charged proteins (green circles with a '-') pass through the column and are collected in the flow-through.

Example:

Purification of Lysozyme (pI ≈ 11) from an extract at pH 7. Lysozyme is positively charged and binds to a cation exchange column. Other proteins with a lower pI will flow through. Lysozyme is then eluted by raising the salt concentration.

Anion Exchange Chromatography 🧪

Separates negatively charged proteins. The stationary phase has a positive charge. Negatively charged proteins bind, while others flow through. Anion exchange chromatography uses a positively charged stationary phase to bind negatively charged proteins (anions). The stationary phase typically contains functional groups like diethylaminoethyl (–DEAE) or quaternary ammonium (–Q). Proteins with net negative charge at the operating pH will bind to the column.

The diagram shows an anion exchange column. The positively charged beads (blue circles with a '+') attract and bind to the negatively charged proteins (green circles with a '-'). The positively charged proteins (red circles with a '+') pass through the column and are collected in the flow-through.

Example:

Purification of a protein with a pI of 4.5 from an extract at pH 7. The protein is negatively charged and binds to an anion exchange column. It is then eluted by increasing the salt concentration.

Separation of serum proteins:

Human serum contains albumin (pI ≈ 4.7) and immunoglobulins (pI ≈ 5.5-8.3). Using anion exchange chromatography at pH 7.4:

  • Albumin (negatively charged) binds to the column.
  • Immunoglobulins (less negative) pass through or elute first.
  • A salt gradient elutes albumin at higher ionic strength.

2. Isoelectric Focusing (IEF)

Separates proteins based on their isoelectric point (pI). The pI is the pH at which a protein's net charge is zero. A pH gradient is created across a gel, and an electric field is applied. Proteins migrate through the gel until they reach a pH equal to their pI, at which point their net charge becomes zero and they stop moving.

This diagram shows a gel with a pH gradient, ranging from acidic (pH 1) on the left to basic (pH 14) on the right. Proteins are loaded and an electric current is applied. Each protein migrates along the gel until it reaches the pH where its net charge is zero, known as its isoelectric point (pI). At this point, it stops moving, resulting in distinct bands based on pI.

Example:

Separation of Hemoglobin variants with different pI values. For example, hemoglobin A (pI ≈ 7.1) and hemoglobin S (pI ≈ 7.2) can be separated in a pH gradient. The small difference in charge at a specific pH causes them to stop at different points, allowing for their resolution.

3. Two-Dimensional Gel Electrophoresis (2DGE)

A powerful technique that combines two separation methods: IEF (by pI) in the first dimension, followed by SDS-PAGE (by size) in the second dimension. This allows for the separation of thousands of proteins from a complex mixture, creating a protein map.

The diagram shows a 2D gel. The horizontal axis represents the isoelectric point (pI), separating proteins by charge from low to high. The vertical axis represents molecular weight, separating proteins by size from low to high. Each dot on the gel is a unique protein separated from the complex mixture. This creates a powerful "map" of the protein sample.

Example:

Analyzing the proteome of a cell before and after a stimulus. 2DGE can reveal which proteins have changed in abundance, providing a detailed picture of the cellular response.
