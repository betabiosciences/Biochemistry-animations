Cardiac Action Potential

Understanding Cardiomyocyte Electrophysiology

Autorhythmicity

Autorhythmicity is the heart's remarkable ability to generate its own electrical impulses without any external nervous stimulation. This intrinsic property means the heart can beat independently, maintaining a regular rhythm even when completely isolated from the body.

Key Features:

  • Spontaneous Depolarization: Pacemaker cells continuously depolarize during diastole.
  • Autonomous Generation: Action potentials fire automatically at regular intervals.
  • Independence: Heart maintains rhythm even when isolated from nervous system.
  • Rate Hierarchy: SA node (60-100 bpm), AV node (40-60 bpm), Bundle of His (20-40 bpm).

Cellular Basis:

Pacemaker cells express If channels (funny currents), L-type and T-type calcium channels, and altered potassium properties.

Types of Cardiomyocytes

The heart contains two distinct types of specialized muscle cells: Contractile cardiomyocytes (99%) and Conducting cardiomyocytes (1%).

1. Contractile Cardiomyocytes (99%)

Function: Generate mechanical force that pumps blood.

  • • Stable resting potential: -80 to -90 mV
  • • Abundant myofibrils
  • • NO autorhythmicity
  • • Action potentials 250-400 ms long

2. Conducting Cardiomyocytes (1%)

Function: Generate and conduct electrical impulses.

  • • Unstable resting potential
  • • Possess autorhythmicity
  • • Rapid conduction velocity (up to 4 m/s)
  • • Primary ion: Calcium

Conduction System

The cardiac conduction system generates electrical impulses and conducts them through the heart in a precisely orchestrated sequence.

SA Node

Location: Superior vena cava and right atrium junction. Role: Primary pacemaker, 60-100 bpm.

AV Node

Location: Right atrium floor. Role: Secondary pacemaker, 40-60 bpm if SA fails.

Bundle of His

Role: Only electrical connection between atria and ventricles.

Bundle Branches and Purkinje Fibers

Purkinje: Terminal branches with fastest conduction (2-4 m/s).

Action Potential: Contractile Cells

Contractile cardiomyocytes generate unique, long-duration action potentials that last 250-400 milliseconds with five distinct phases.

Phase 0: Rapid Depolarization

Duration: 1-2 ms. Current: Fast Na+ influx.

Phase 1: Early Repolarization

Duration: 10-20 ms. Mechanism: Na+ channels inactivate.

Phase 2: Plateau

Duration: 100-200 ms. Balance: Ca2+ influx equals K+ efflux.

Phase 3: Repolarization

Duration: 100-150 ms. Mechanism: Ca2+ channels close, K+ efflux dominates.

Phase 4: Resting Potential

Potential: Stable -90 mV. Key: NO spontaneous depolarization.

Action Potential: Conducting Cells

Conducting (pacemaker) cells lack a stable resting potential and show continuous spontaneous depolarization during Phase 4.

Phase 0: Slow Depolarization

Current: CALCIUM (not fast Na+). Speed: 100-200 mV/s (slower).

Phase 3: Repolarization

Key: Returns to only -60 mV (not -80 mV).

Phase 4: Spontaneous Diastolic Depolarization

Key: UNSTABLE, spontaneous without stimulus.

Pacemaker vs Contractile:

Pacemakers lack Phase 1 and 2 (no plateau). Phase 4 is UNSTABLE and spontaneous.

Electrocardiogram (ECG)

The electrocardiogram is the most important diagnostic tool in cardiology, recording the electrical activity of the heart from the body surface.

ECG Waves and Intervals:

P Wave: Atrial depolarization. Duration 80-120 ms.
PR Interval: 120-200 ms. Atrial depolarization plus AV delay.
QRS Complex: Ventricular depolarization. Duration 80-120 ms.
ST Segment: Should be flat. Elevation suggests MI.
T Wave: Ventricular repolarization.

Abnormal Findings:

ST elevation suggests MI. ST depression suggests ischemia. Prolonged PR indicates AV block.
