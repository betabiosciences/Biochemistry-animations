Protein Translation Components

1. mRNA - Messenger RNA

Messenger RNA (mRNA) serves as the template for protein synthesis, carrying genetic information from DNA to the ribosome.

Monocistronic vs Polycistronic mRNA

Monocistronic mRNA: Encodes a single protein. This is characteristic of eukaryotic cells where each mRNA molecule typically produces one polypeptide chain.

Polycistronic mRNA: Contains multiple coding regions (cistrons) that can be translated into several proteins. Common in prokaryotes, these mRNAs often encode functionally related proteins in an operon.

Prokaryotic mRNA Features

Shine-Dalgarno Sequence: A ribosomal binding site in prokaryotic mRNA located 6-10 nucleotides upstream of the start codon (AUG). This purine-rich sequence (5'-AGGAGG-3') base pairs with complementary sequences on the 16S rRNA of the small ribosomal subunit, facilitating proper initiation complex formation.

Eukaryotic mRNA Features

Kozak Sequence: Plays a critical role in translation initiation in eukaryotes. The consensus sequence (GCC)GCC(A/G)CCAUGG enhances recognition of the start codon by the ribosome. The adenosine at position -3 and guanosine at +4 (relative to AUG at +1 to +3) are particularly important for efficient initiation.

Additional eukaryotic mRNA features include:

  • 5' cap structure (7-methylguanosine) for stability and ribosome binding
  • 3' poly-A tail for stability and nuclear export
  • Splicing of introns to form mature mRNA

2. tRNA - Transfer RNA

Transfer RNA (tRNA) serves as the adapter molecule that links specific amino acids to their corresponding codons in mRNA during translation.

Structure of tRNA

tRNAs share a common secondary and tertiary structure despite sequence variations:

  • Typically 73-93 nucleotides in length
  • Contains numerous modified bases
  • Folds into an L-shaped tertiary structure

Cloverleaf Structure

The cloverleaf model represents tRNA's secondary structure with four main arms:

  1. Acceptor arm: Formed by pairing of the 5' and 3' ends, ending with the CCA sequence where the amino acid attaches
  2. D arm: Contains dihydrouridine residues
  3. Anticodon arm: Contains the anticodon triplet that base pairs with mRNA codons
  4. TΨC arm: Contains the sequence TΨC (ribothymidine-pseudouridine-cytidine)

Acceptor Arm and Anticodon Arm

Acceptor Arm: The 3' end of tRNA always terminates with the sequence CCA. The amino acid is esterified to the 3' hydroxyl group of the terminal adenosine.

Anticodon Arm: Contains the anticodon loop with three nucleotides that form complementary base pairs with the corresponding codon in mRNA. This ensures the correct amino acid is incorporated into the growing polypeptide chain.

Base Modifications in tRNA

tRNAs contain the highest proportion of modified nucleotides of any RNA type:

  • Dihydrouridine (D): In the D-arm, increases flexibility
  • Pseudouridine (Ψ): Stabilizes tRNA structure
  • Ribothymidine (T): In the TΨC arm
  • Inosine (I): Often found in the wobble position of the anticodon, allowing pairing with multiple codons
  • Queuosine: A complex modification in the anticodon loop

These modifications enhance tRNA stability, facilitate proper folding, and expand codon recognition capabilities.

3. Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetase

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRS) are enzymes that catalyze the attachment of specific amino acids to their corresponding tRNAs, a process known as tRNA charging.

tRNA Charging Mechanism

The charging reaction occurs in two steps:

  1. Amino acid activation: Amino acid + ATP → aminoacyl-AMP + PPi
  2. tRNA charging: Aminoacyl-AMP + tRNA → aminoacyl-tRNA + AMP

This process is highly specific, with each of the 20 standard amino acids having at least one dedicated aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase.

Measures to Improve Charging Accuracy

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases employ multiple strategies to ensure high fidelity:

  • Double-sieve mechanism: Larger amino acids are excluded from the active site based on size (first sieve), while similarly sized amino acids are discriminated against in a separate editing site (second sieve)
  • Structural proofreading: Conformational changes after initial binding help discriminate between similar amino acids
  • Kinetic proofreading: Incorrect products are hydrolyzed more rapidly than correct ones

Proofreading by Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetase

Proofreading occurs through two main mechanisms:

  1. Pre-transfer editing: Hydrolysis of the non-cognate aminoacyl-AMP before transfer to tRNA
  2. Post-transfer editing: Hydrolysis of the mischarged aminoacyl-tRNA after transfer

These editing mechanisms reduce error rates from approximately 1 in 100 to 1 in 10,000-100,000, ensuring translational fidelity.

4. Ribosome

The ribosome is a complex molecular machine that catalyzes protein synthesis by translating the genetic code in mRNA into a polypeptide chain.

Structure

Ribosomes consist of two subunits of unequal size:

  • Prokaryotic ribosomes: 70S (30S small subunit + 50S large subunit)
  • Eukaryotic ribosomes: 80S (40S small subunit + 60S large subunit)

Both subunits are composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal proteins. The rRNA constitutes about 2/3 of the ribosome's mass and performs key catalytic functions.

Functional Centers

Decoding Centre: Located in the small subunit where mRNA codons are matched with tRNA anticodons. This center ensures accurate codon-anticodon pairing through conformational monitoring.

Peptidyl Transferase Center: Located in the large subunit, this is the site of peptide bond formation. Remarkably, this catalytic activity is performed by rRNA, making the ribosome a ribozyme.

Sites on Ribosomes

Ribosomes contain three main tRNA binding sites:

  • A site (Aminoacyl site): Binds incoming aminoacyl-tRNA
  • P site (Peptidyl site): Holds the tRNA with the growing polypeptide chain
  • E site (Exit site): Contains the deacylated tRNA before it leaves the ribosome

Additionally, the ribosome has an mRNA binding channel and exit tunnel for the nascent polypeptide chain.

Lifecycle During Translation

The ribosome undergoes a dynamic cycle during protein synthesis:

  1. Initiation: Small subunit binds mRNA and initiator tRNA, followed by large subunit joining
  2. Elongation: Cyclical process of aminoacyl-tRNA binding, peptide bond formation, and translocation
  3. Termination: Recognition of stop codon leads to release of completed polypeptide
  4. Recycling: Ribosomal subunits dissociate and are available for new rounds of translation

This cycle requires several protein factors (initiation, elongation, and release factors) and energy from GTP hydrolysis.
