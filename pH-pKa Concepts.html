pH and pKa in Biomolecules

Understanding acid-base properties and their critical role in amino acids, proteins, and biological systems

What is pH?

pH is a measure of the acidity or basicity of a solution. It's defined as the negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration:

pH Definition

pH = -log10[H+]

Where [H+] is the concentration of hydrogen ions in moles per liter.

The pH scale ranges from 0 to 14:

  • pH < 7: Acidic solution (higher H+ concentration)
  • pH = 7: Neutral solution
  • pH > 7: Basic (alkaline) solution

Biological Significance

Most biological processes occur within a narrow pH range (typically 6.5-7.5). Enzymes, proteins, and cellular structures are highly sensitive to pH changes:

  • Blood pH is tightly regulated at 7.4
  • Lysosomes function at pH 4.5-5.0
  • Mitochondrial matrix has pH ~7.8
  • pH affects protein structure, enzyme activity, and membrane function

What is pKa?

pKa is a measure of acid strength. It indicates how readily a molecule donates a proton (H+). pKa is defined as:

pKa Definition

pKa = -log10Ka

Where Ka is the acid dissociation constant: Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA]

Key characteristics of pKa:

  • Lower pKa = Stronger acid (more likely to donate proton)
  • Higher pKa = Weaker acid (less likely to donate proton)
  • At pH = pKa, 50% of the acid is dissociated

The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

This equation relates pH, pKa, and the ratio of conjugate base to acid:

pH = pKa + log10([A-]/[HA])

Here, [A⁻] represents the concentration of the conjugate base (the deprotonated form) and [HA] represents the concentration of the weak acid (the protonated form).

pH-pKa Relationship Explained

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation reveals that the ionization state of a molecule depends on the difference between pH and pKa:

  • When pH < pKa, the environment is acidic, and the molecule tends to remain protonated (HA)
  • When pH > pKa, the environment is basic, and the molecule tends to lose its proton (A⁻)
  • At pH = pKa, exactly half of the molecules are protonated and half are deprotonated

Memory Tricks for Ionization States

The "1 Unit Rule"

When pH is one unit below pKa, the ratio is 1:10 (90% protonated). When pH is one unit above pKa, the ratio is 10:1 (90% deprotonated).

Protonation Mnemonic

"Low pH, proton high" - At low pH relative to pKa, the protonated form (HA) dominates.

"High pH, proton bye" - At high pH relative to pKa, the deprotonated form (A⁻) dominates.

Charge Prediction

For acids: When pH < pKa, the group is protonated and neutral. When pH > pKa, it's deprotonated and negatively charged.

For bases: When pH < pKa, the group is protonated and positively charged. When pH > pKa, it's deprotonated and neutral.

Interpreting the Ionization Graph

This graph illustrates the percentage of protonated (HA) and deprotonated (A⁻) forms of a weak acid as a function of pH.

  • When the pH is significantly lower than the pKa, the environment is acidic, and the molecule is predominantly in its protonated (HA) form. The red curve (Protonated) is high, and the blue curve (Deprotonated) is low.
  • As the pH increases towards the pKa, the molecule begins to lose protons. The percentage of the protonated form decreases, and the percentage of the deprotonated form increases.
  • At the pH equal to the pKa, the concentrations of the protonated (HA) and deprotonated (A⁻) forms are exactly equal (50% each). This is indicated by the intersection of the two curves and the vertical dashed line.
  • When the pH is significantly higher than the pKa, the environment is basic, and the molecule is predominantly in its deprotonated (A⁻) form. The blue curve (Deprotonated) is high, and the red curve (Protonated) is low.
  • Conversely, if the pH decreases from a high value towards the pKa, the deprotonated form will gain protons, increasing the concentration of the protonated form.

pH and pKa in Amino Acids

Amino acids are amphoteric molecules that contain both acidic (carboxyl) and basic (amino) groups. Their ionization states change with pH:

Low pH (Acidic)

Both groups are protonated:

NH3+ — CHR — COOH

Net charge: +1

Intermediate pH

Zwitterion form:

NH3+ — CHR — COO-

Net charge: 0

High pH (Basic)

Both groups deprotonated:

NH2 — CHR — COO-

Net charge: -1

Biological Significance

Protein Structure and Function

pH and pKa values profoundly impact proteins:

  • Enzyme catalysis: Active sites often contain acidic/basic residues with precisely tuned pKa values
  • Protein folding: Ionizable groups influence folding and stability
  • Ligand binding: pH affects binding affinities and specificities
  • Membrane transport: pH gradients drive transport mechanisms

Buffers in Biological Systems

Biological systems maintain pH using buffer systems:

  • Bicarbonate buffer: Maintains blood pH (pKa=6.1)
  • Phosphate buffer: Important in intracellular fluid (pKa=6.8)
  • Protein buffers: Histidine residues (pKa≈6.0) are particularly important

pH and Drug Design

Understanding pKa is critical for pharmaceutical development:

  • Influences drug solubility and absorption
  • Affects drug distribution across membranes
  • Determines binding to target proteins
  • Impacts metabolic stability
