Thermodynamics in Biochemistry

Exploring the Energetics of Life

Thermodynamic Spaces

In thermodynamics, we divide the universe into two parts to study energy transfer: the system and the surroundings. The system is the specific part of the universe we are interested in (e.g., a cell, a test tube reaction). The surroundings are everything else. Together, the system and its surroundings constitute the universe.

System Boundary

The boundary is the real or imaginary surface that separates the system from its surroundings. The nature of this boundary determines how the system interacts with its surroundings, leading to three types of systems:

  • Open System: Can exchange both energy (heat, work) and matter with the surroundings. Living organisms are classic examples of open systems.
  • Closed System: Can exchange energy but not matter with the surroundings. A sealed test tube is a good approximation.
  • Isolated System: Cannot exchange either energy or matter with the surroundings. A perfectly insulated container (like a thermos flask) is an approximation.

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

This law provides the basis for temperature measurement. It states:

"If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other."

This means if system A is at the same temperature as system C, and system B is also at the same temperature as system C, then systems A and B are at the same temperature. This allows us to use a thermometer (system C) to measure and compare the temperatures of other objects.

The First Law of Thermodynamics

Also known as the law of conservation of energy, it states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system is constant.

The change in the internal energy (ΔU) of a system is equal to the heat (q) added to the system plus the work (w) done by the system.

ΔU = q + w

Enthalpy (H)

In biological systems, reactions often occur at constant pressure. Enthalpy (H) is a more convenient measure of energy change under these conditions. It represents the total heat content of a system. The change in enthalpy (ΔH) is the heat absorbed or released in a reaction at constant pressure.

H = U + PV

Exothermic (ΔH < 0): The reaction releases heat into the surroundings (e.g., combustion, ATP hydrolysis). The products have lower enthalpy than the reactants.

Endothermic (ΔH > 0): The reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings (e.g., photosynthesis, melting ice). The products have higher enthalpy than the reactants.

Isenthalpic (ΔH = 0): No heat is exchanged.

The Second Law of Thermodynamics

This law deals with the direction of spontaneous processes. It states:

"In any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings) always increases."

This means that processes naturally proceed towards a state of greater disorder or randomness. It introduces the crucial concept of entropy (S).

Spontaneity of Reactions

The second law helps us define whether a reaction will happen on its own, without external energy input.

Exergonic Reaction: A reaction that proceeds spontaneously and releases free energy. These reactions have a net "downhill" energy change.

Endergonic Reaction: A reaction that is not spontaneous and requires an input of free energy to proceed. These reactions have a net "uphill" energy change.

Entropy (S)

Entropy is a measure of the randomness, disorder, or dispersal of energy in a system. A system with higher entropy is more disordered.

Microstates and Macrostates

A macrostate is the overall state of a system (e.g., its temperature, pressure). A microstate is a specific arrangement of all the individual particles (atoms, molecules) that results in that macrostate. A highly disordered macrostate has a vast number of possible microstates. Entropy is related to the number of accessible microstates (W)—the specific arrangements of atoms and molecules—that correspond to the system's overall observable macrostate (its temperature, pressure, etc.). A higher number of possible arrangements means greater disorder and thus higher entropy. For instance, when a gas expands to fill a container, it has vastly more possible positions for its molecules than when it was confined to a small corner, so its entropy increases.

The Boltzmann equation relates entropy (S) to the number of microstates (W):

S = kB ln(W)

Where kB is the Boltzmann constant.

Entropy and Temperature

Increasing the temperature of a system increases the kinetic energy of its particles, allowing them to access more microstates and thus increasing the system's entropy. The change in entropy (ΔS) for a reversible process at constant temperature is related to the heat transferred (qrev):

ΔS = qrev / T

Summary Points

  • Equilibrium state of a system is a state of maximum randomness.
  • All non-equilibrium states are more ordered than the equilibrium state.
  • All systems have an inherent tendency to move towards equilibrium.
  • A system at equilibrium will show no tendency for any change.
  • The further the state of a system is from equilibrium, the greater is the tendency for change.

Entropy thus:

  • Defines the randomness/disorderness of energy distribution in a system.
  • Entropy is said to be high when the energy is randomly distributed.
  • When energy is concentrated entropy tends to decrease.
  • Highest entropic state of a system is at their equilibrium.

Gibbs Free Energy (G)

Gibbs free energy (G) is the most important thermodynamic concept for biochemists. It represents the portion of a system's total energy that is available to do useful work at constant temperature and pressure. It combines enthalpy and entropy into a single value to predict spontaneity.

ΔG = ΔH - TΔS

ΔG < 0: The reaction is spontaneous (exergonic). It will proceed in the forward direction.

ΔG > 0: The reaction is non-spontaneous (endergonic). It requires energy input to proceed; the reverse reaction is spontaneous.

ΔG = 0: The system is at equilibrium. There is no net change in the concentrations of reactants and products.

Life and Entropy

Living organisms are highly ordered, seemingly defying the second law. However, they maintain their low-entropy state by increasing the entropy of their surroundings (e.g., by releasing heat and simple waste products like CO2). The total entropy of the universe still increases.

Standard Free Energy (ΔG°') and Reaction Quotient (Q)

The actual free energy change (ΔG) depends on the concentrations of reactants and products. This is calculated using the reaction quotient (Q):

ΔG = ΔG°' + RT ln(Q)

Where R is the gas constant, T is temperature in Kelvin, and ΔG°' is the standard free energy change under standard biochemical conditions (pH 7, 1 M concentrations).

Units

  • Enthalpy (ΔH): kJ/mol or kcal/mol
  • Entropy (ΔS): J/(mol·K) or cal/(mol·K)
  • Free Energy (ΔG): kJ/mol or kcal/mol

Equilibrium and Temperature

At equilibrium, ΔG = 0, and the reaction quotient Q equals the equilibrium constant Keq. This gives a crucial relationship:

ΔG°' = -RT ln(Keq)

Van't Hoff Equation & Plot

The Van't Hoff equation shows how the equilibrium constant (Keq) changes with temperature (T), depending on the enthalpy change (ΔH°) of the reaction:

ln(Keq) = -ΔH°/RT + ΔS°/R

This is in the form of a straight line equation (y = mx + c). A Van't Hoff plot of ln(Keq) vs 1/T gives a straight line with a slope of -ΔH°/R.

Exothermic (ΔH < 0): The slope is positive. As temperature increases (1/T decreases), Keq decreases. High temp disfavors the reaction.

Endothermic (ΔH > 0): The slope is negative. As temperature increases (1/T decreases), Keq increases. High temp favors the reaction.

Le Chatelier's Principle

This principle is a qualitative consequence of these relationships. It states that if a change is applied to a system at equilibrium, the system will shift in a direction that counteracts the change. For temperature, this means adding heat to an exothermic reaction will shift it towards reactants, while adding heat to an endothermic reaction will shift it towards products.

Thermodynamics of Coupled Reactions

Many essential biochemical reactions are endergonic (unfavorable, ΔG > 0). To drive these reactions, cells couple them with highly exergonic (favorable, ΔG < 0) reactions. The free energy changes are additive. As long as the overall ΔG for the coupled process is negative, the entire process will be spontaneous.

Example from Glycolysis

The first step of glycolysis, the phosphorylation of glucose, is endergonic:

Glucose + Pi → Glucose-6-phosphate + H2O     ΔG°' = +13.8 kJ/mol

This reaction won't happen on its own. The cell couples it to the highly exergonic hydrolysis of ATP:

ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi     ΔG°' = -30.5 kJ/mol

The overall coupled reaction, catalyzed by the enzyme hexokinase, is:

Glucose + ATP → Glucose-6-phosphate + ADP

Overall ΔG°' = (+13.8) + (-30.5) = -16.7 kJ/mol

Because the net free energy change is negative, the phosphorylation of glucose proceeds spontaneously within the cell.

The Third Law of Thermodynamics

The third law provides a reference point for determining absolute entropy. It states:

"The entropy of a perfect, pure crystalline substance is zero at absolute zero temperature (0 Kelvin)."

At 0 K, all motion of particles ceases, and if the substance is a perfect crystal, there is only one possible arrangement of its atoms (W=1). According to the Boltzmann equation, S = kB ln(1) = 0.

This law is significant because it allows us to calculate the absolute entropy of a substance at any temperature above 0 K, which is impossible for enthalpy or free energy. This provides a fundamental baseline for all entropy calculations.
