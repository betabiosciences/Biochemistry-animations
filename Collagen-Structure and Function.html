Structure Essentials

The Basic Blueprint

Tripeptide Repeat: The primary structure of a collagen polypeptide is a repeating sequence of three amino acids: Gly-X-Y.

  • Glycine (Gly): With only a hydrogen atom as its side chain, it's the smallest amino acid. This is critical for allowing the three helical chains to pack into a very tight superhelix.
  • X (often Proline): Its rigid ring structure helps form the helical turns of the individual polypeptide chain.
  • Y (often Hydroxyproline): A modified version of proline. Its hydroxyl (-OH) group is vital for forming hydrogen bonds that stabilize the final triple helix.

This unique, repetitive sequence forces each polypeptide chain into a left-handed helix called a polyproline II helix, which is more extended than a typical alpha-helix.

Biosynthesis Roadmap

Tap each step to learn what happens from gene to tough fibrils.

1 Synthesis of preprocollagen (RER)
⌄

Ribosomes make prepro-α chains with signal peptides. After entry into the rough ER, signal peptides are removed → procollagen α-chains.

2 Hydroxylation (needs Vitamin C)
⌄

Selected proline and lysine residues are hydroxylated by prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases. These enzymes require vitamin C (ascorbate), Fe²⁺, and O₂. Hydroxyproline is crucial for helix stability.

3 Glycosylation and helix assembly
⌄

Some hydroxylysine residues are glycosylated (glucose/galactose). Three α-chains align via C-terminal propeptides and zip up into a triple helix → procollagen.

4 Golgi packaging & secretion
⌄

Procollagen is packaged in the Golgi into secretory vesicles and released outside the cell along microtubules to the plasma membrane.

5 Propeptide cleavage → tropocollagen
⌄

Outside the cell, N- and C-procollagen peptidases remove terminal propeptides, yielding tropocollagen able to laterally assemble.

6 Fibrillogenesis & cross-linking
⌄

Tropocollagen self-assembles into striated fibrils in a quarter-staggered pattern; lysyl oxidase then initiates covalent cross-links to strengthen the fibrils.

Vitamin C & Scurvy

Tap a panel to reveal details.

🍊 Why Vitamin C?
⌄

Vitamin C (ascorbate) keeps iron in the active form within prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases. Without it, hydroxylation of proline and lysine fails, weakening hydrogen bonding and destabilizing the triple helix.

🩸 Scurvy
⌄

Insufficient vitamin C → under-hydroxylated collagen → fragile connective tissues. Findings include bleeding gums, easy bruising, poor wound healing, corkscrew hairs, subperiosteal hemorrhage, and bone pain.

🧷 Helix instability
⌄

Hydroxyproline enables hydrogen bonds that stabilize the triple helix at body temperature. When hydroxylation is impaired, the helix loosens and becomes unstable.

Cross-linking Strength (Lysyl Oxidase)

Cross-links between collagen molecules give tensile strength. Failure → fragile tissues (lathyrism).

What lysyl oxidase does
  • Converts lysine/hydroxylysine into aldehydes (allysine), enabling covalent cross-links.
  • Requires copper as a cofactor; copper deficiency weakens cross-linking.
  • Acts after fibrils assemble outside the cell to mature the matrix.
🌱
Lathyrism

Lathyrism is a syndrome of weak connective tissues due to impaired cross-links, historically linked to Lathyrus (sweet pea) seed exposure.

β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN)
  • Mechanism: irreversible inhibitor of lysyl oxidase; prevents allysine formation, blocking cross-links.
  • Use: research tool to induce lathyrism; not a treatment in patients due to toxicity.
  • Effects: leads to fragile vessels, skeletal deformities, and poor wound strength when exposure occurs.

Bone, skin, tendon, ligaments, dentin. High tensile strength.

Type III
[α1(III)]₃

Reticular fibers: vessels, skin, uterus, granulation tissue.

Type IV
Network-forming

Basement membranes (lamina densa). Mesh-like, not fibrillar.

Type V
Fibril-associated

Regulates fibril diameter; in placenta and interstitial tissues.
