Mitochondrial Protein Targeting

1. General Features & Overview

Mitochondria are double-membrane organelles that contain their own genome, yet they synthesize only a fraction of their proteins (approx. 1%). The remaining 99% are nuclear-encoded, synthesized on cytosolic polysomes, and imported post-translationally.

Key Concept: Import is post-translational and requires proteins to remain in an unfolded state via cytosolic chaperones.

Essential Components

  • Signal Sequences: Usually an N-terminal presequence (20-50 amino acids) forming an amphipathic α-helix (positive charges on one side, hydrophobic on the other).
  • Chaperones: Cytosolic Hsp70 and MSF (Mitochondrial Import Stimulation Factor) keep precursors unfolded.
  • Translocases:
    - TOM Complex: Translocase of the Outer Membrane.
    - TIM Complex: Translocase of the Inner Membrane.

2. Targeting to the Matrix

This is the "General Import Pathway" utilized by the majority of cleavable preproteins.

Mechanism Steps

  1. Recognition: The TOM complex (specifically receptors Tom20 and Tom22) recognizes the N-terminal amphipathic helix.
  2. Translocation across OMM: The protein passes through the Tom40 pore.
  3. Translocation across IMM: The positive presequence is driven through the TIM23 complex by the electrochemical potential (Δψ) across the inner membrane.
  4. The Motor: The Presequence Translocase-Associated Motor (PAM) complex, driven by mitochondrial Hsp70 and ATP hydrolysis, pulls the protein into the matrix.
  5. Processing: The MPP (Mitochondrial Processing Peptidase) cleaves the signal sequence.
Energy Requirement: Matrix targeting requires both ATP (cytosolic and matrix) and the Proton Motive Force (Membrane Potential).

3. Targeting to Inner Membrane

Proteins destined for the Inner Mitochondrial Membrane (IMM) utilize three distinct sorting pathways depending on their structure and function.

Path A: Stop-Transfer (TIM23)

Proteins contain an N-terminal signal followed by a hydrophobic stop-transfer sequence. TIM23 arrests translocation, and the protein is laterally released into the lipid bilayer.

Path B: Conservative Sorting (OXA)

Used by some OxPhos subunits. The protein enters the matrix completely and is then re-inserted into the IMM from the matrix side by the OXA complex. This mimics bacterial insertion.

Path C: Carrier Pathway (TIM22)

Used by metabolite carriers (e.g., ADP/ATP carrier) which lack cleavable signals. They have internal targeting signals. They cross TOM, navigate the intermembrane space via Tiny Tims (chaperones), and are inserted by the TIM22 complex.

4. Targeting to Intermembrane Space

The Intermembrane Space (IMS), or peri-mitochondrial space, houses proteins involved in transport and apoptosis. There are two primary mechanisms.

1. The MIA Pathway (Redox trap)

Used by small, cysteine-rich proteins (e.g., Tim9, Tim10). They enter via TOM. In the IMS, the MIA40 receptor forms a transient disulfide bond with the protein. ERV1 completes the folding by oxidizing the protein. This "locks" the folded protein in the IMS preventing back-sliding.

2. Bipartite Signal Pathway

Proteins (like Cytochrome b2) have a matrix-targeting signal AND an IMS-sorting signal. They are arrested in the TIM23 complex (Stop-Transfer), leaving the bulk of the protein in the IMS. A protease cleaves the protein, releasing it soluble into the IMS.

5. Targeting to Outer Membrane

Proteins resident in the Outer Mitochondrial Membrane (OMM) fall into two main categories: Signal-anchored proteins and Beta-barrel proteins.

Signal-Anchored Proteins

Proteins with N-terminal signal-anchor sequences (e.g., Tom20, Tom70) are inserted directly by the MIM complex (Mitochondrial Import Machinery) without entering the translocation pore deeply.

Beta-Barrel Proteins

Porins (e.g., VDAC/Tom40) are synthesized as precursors.
1. They cross the OMM via the TOM complex into the intermembrane space.
2. Chaperones (Tiny Tims) bind them to prevent aggregation.
3. They are delivered to the SAM Complex (Sorting and Assembly Machinery) which inserts and folds them into the outer membrane from the IMS side.

Evolutionary Note: The SAM complex is homologous to the BAM complex in Gram-negative bacteria, reflecting mitochondria's endosymbiotic origin.
