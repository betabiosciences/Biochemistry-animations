DNA Repair Mechanisms

1. Photoreactivation (Photolyases)

Photoreactivation is a light-dependent DNA repair mechanism that directly reverses UV-induced DNA damage, specifically cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6-4 photoproducts. This process is unique as it requires visible light (300-500 nm) and doesn't involve DNA backbone cleavage.

Photolyase Enzymes: Structure and Function

Photolyases are flavoproteins that contain two essential chromophores:

  • FADH⁻ (fully reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide): The catalytic cofactor that transfers electrons to the damaged DNA
  • Light-harvesting chromophore: MTHF (methenyltetrahydrofolate) absorbs blue/UV-A light

Key Insight: Photolyases are evolutionarily ancient enzymes found in bacteria, fungi, plants, and many animals, but interestingly, placental mammals have lost this repair pathway, relying instead on nucleotide excision repair.

CPD Photolyase

Structure and Specificity

CPD photolyase specifically recognizes and repairs cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers, which form when adjacent pyrimidine bases (thymine or cytosine) become covalently linked by UV radiation.

Catalytic Mechanism

The repair process occurs through the following steps:

  1. DNA Scanning and Recognition: CPD photolyase scans DNA nonspecifically until it encounters a cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer through a unique flipping mechanism that extrudes the damaged bases from the DNA helix into the enzyme's active site.
  2. Light Absorption: The antenna chromophore (MTHF) absorbs a photon of blue/UV-A light (300-500 nm).
  3. Energy Transfer: The excitation energy is transferred to FADH⁻ via fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) with high efficiency (≈90%).
  4. Electron Transfer: Photoexcited FADH⁻ donates an electron to the pyrimidine dimer, generating a transient anion radical that destabilizes the cyclobutane ring.
  5. Bond Cleavage: The cyclobutane ring undergoes splitting via a concerted mechanism, restoring the two original pyrimidine bases.
  6. Electron Return and Product Release: The electron returns to FADH, regenerating the active enzyme, and the repaired DNA is released.

Evolutionary Distribution

CPD photolyases are widely distributed across all three domains of life:

  • Bacteria: Present in many species including E. coli (encoded by the phr gene)
  • Archaea: Found in some halophilic and thermophilic species
  • Eukarya: Present in plants, fungi, insects, fish, amphibians, and marsupials

Advanced Detail: The quantum yield of CPD photolyase is remarkably high (≈0.9), meaning nearly every photon absorbed results in a repaired dimer, making it one of the most efficient enzymes known. The enzyme achieves this through optimal positioning of cofactors and precise electron transfer pathways.

6-4 Photolyase

Structure and Specificity

6-4 photolyase specifically repairs 6-4 photoproducts, which form when UV radiation induces a covalent bond between the C6 position of one pyrimidine and the C4 position of an adjacent pyrimidine. These lesions are more distorting to DNA structure than CPDs.

Catalytic Mechanism

The repair mechanism for 6-4 photoproducts differs significantly from CPD repair:

  1. Substrate Recognition: 6-4 photolyase recognizes the more structurally distorted 6-4 photoproduct through specific interactions with the altered bases.
  2. Light Absorption and Energy Transfer: Similar to CPD photolyase, the antenna chromophore absorbs light and transfers energy to FADH⁻.
  3. Electron Transfer and Intermediate Formation: FADH⁻ transfers an electron to the 6-4 photoproduct, generating an anion radical. This leads to formation of an oxetane or azetidine intermediate.
  4. Bond Rearrangement: The unstable intermediate undergoes spontaneous rearrangement, breaking the covalent bonds between the pyrimidines.
  5. Restoration of Original Bases: The original pyrimidine bases are restored, though sometimes with altered tautomeric states that gradually convert to the normal forms.

Evolutionary Distribution

6-4 photolyases have a more restricted distribution than CPD photolyases:

  • Bacteria: Less common than CPD photolyases
  • Eukarya: Found in insects, fish, amphibians, and some plants, but absent in fungi

Advanced Detail: Some organisms possess "photolyase-like" proteins that share structural similarities with photolyases but function as circadian rhythm regulators (cryptochromes) rather than DNA repair enzymes. This highlights the evolutionary repurposing of this protein fold.

2. Suicide Enzymes

Suicide enzymes, also known as suicide inactivators or mechanism-based inactivators, are enzymes that catalyze their own irreversible inactivation during the processing of their substrates.

Definition and Characteristics

Suicide enzymes are unique in that they:

  • Recognize and bind specific substrate molecules
  • Initiate a catalytic reaction that transforms the substrate
  • Generate a highly reactive intermediate during catalysis
  • This reactive intermediate covalently modifies and permanently inactivates the enzyme

Role in DNA Repair

In DNA repair contexts, suicide enzymes play crucial roles:

  1. O⁶-alkylguanine-DNA alkyltransferase (AGT): Directly transfers alkyl groups from damaged DNA to its own cysteine residue, inactivating itself in the process

Clinical Relevance: The suicide enzyme O⁶-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) is a critical determinant in the effectiveness of alkylating chemotherapeutic agents. Tumors with low MGMT expression are more susceptible to these drugs.

3. Alpha Ketoglutarate Dependent Dioxygenase

Alpha-ketoglutarate (αKG)-dependent dioxygenases are a large enzyme family that utilizes molecular oxygen and α-ketoglutarate to catalyze hydroxylation reactions, with important roles in DNA repair, particularly for alkylation damage.

Enzyme Family Overview

These enzymes share a common reaction mechanism:

  • Require Fe²⁺ as a cofactor
  • Utilize α-ketoglutarate as a cosubstrate
  • Consume one molecule of O₂ per reaction cycle
  • Generate succinate and CO₂ as byproducts

DNA Repair Applications

In DNA repair, the most significant αKG-dependent dioxygenases are:

  1. AlkB homologs (ALKBH1-8 in humans): Directly reverse alkylation damage by oxidative demethylation

Key Mechanism: ALKBH2 and ALKBH3 repair 1-methyladenine and 3-methylcytosine lesions by oxidizing the methyl group to hydroxymethyl, which spontaneously releases formaldehyde, restoring the undamaged base.
