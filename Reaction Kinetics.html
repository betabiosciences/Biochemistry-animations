Reaction Kinetics

Collision Theory • Transition State Theory

1. Collision Theory

Collision theory provides a straightforward model for understanding how chemical reactions occur. Its core principle is that reactant particles must collide with each other for a reaction to take place, but not all collisions are effective.

At its core, collision theory is a model that quantitatively explains the rates of chemical reactions by relating them to the frequency of collisions between reactant molecules. The advanced treatment refines the basic model by incorporating molecular speed distributions, orientation effects, and energy thresholds with greater mathematical rigor.

The fundamental postulate is:

The rate of a bimolecular elementary reaction is proportional to:

  • The Collision Frequency (Z): The number of collisions per unit time per unit volume.
  • The Fraction of Collisions with Sufficient Energy (f): The fraction of colliding pairs that have kinetic energy greater than or equal to the activation energy (Ea).
  • The Steric Factor (p): The fraction of collisions with the correct molecular orientation.

Thus, the rate constant k is given by:

k = p ⋅ Z ⋅ f

The activation energy, Ea, is the minimum amount of energy that reacting particles must possess to overcome the energy barrier and form products. Think of it like a hill that a hiker needs to climb before they can get to the valley on the other side. A higher activation energy means a steeper hill, and therefore fewer particles will have enough energy to make it over, resulting in a slower reaction rate.

The rate law is a crucial mathematical expression that relates the rate of a reaction to the concentration of its reactants. For a generic reaction aA + bB → products, the rate law is:

Rate = k[A]m[B]n

Here, [A] and [B] are the molar concentrations, k is the rate constant, and m and n are the reaction orders with respect to A and B. It is important to remember that the exponents m and n are determined experimentally and are not necessarily equal to the stoichiometric coefficients a and b.

The rate constant, k, is a proportionality constant that links the concentrations of reactants to the reaction rate. A larger value of k signifies a faster reaction. The rate constant is unique for a given reaction and is highly dependent on temperature. Its units change depending on the overall reaction order.

The order of a reaction is the exponent of a reactant's concentration in the rate law. The overall reaction order is the sum of all the exponents (m+n). It describes the dependence of the reaction rate on concentration. For example, if doubling the concentration of a reactant doubles the rate, the reaction is first order with respect to that reactant. The order of a reaction can be zero, an integer, or even a fraction, and it must be determined by experiment.

Molecularity is a theoretical concept that refers to the number of molecules, atoms, or ions that participate in an elementary reaction step (a single step in a reaction mechanism). Unlike reaction order, which is a property of the overall reaction and determined experimentally, molecularity is an intrinsic property of an elementary step. It can be unimolecular (one particle), bimolecular (two), or termolecular (three).

Zero-order reaction: The rate is independent of reactant concentration. The rate is constant and equal to k. Example: The decomposition of dinitrogen monoxide on a gold surface.
Rate = k[N2O]0 = k

First-order reaction: The rate is directly proportional to the concentration of a single reactant. Example: The radioactive decay of carbon-14.
Rate = k[14C]1

Second-order reaction: The rate is proportional to the square of a single reactant's concentration or the product of two reactant concentrations. Example: The decomposition of nitrogen dioxide.
Rate = k[NO2]2

The Arrhenius equation mathematically relates the rate constant (k) to temperature. It is given by:

k = A e-Ea/RT

Where A is the pre-exponential factor (or frequency factor), representing the frequency of correctly oriented collisions. The exponential term, e-Ea/RT, is the fraction of collisions with sufficient energy. The equation clearly shows that increasing temperature (T) or decreasing activation energy (Ea) will increase the rate constant, and thus the reaction rate.

A linear form of the Arrhenius equation can be used to find the activation energy experimentally. By taking the natural logarithm of both sides, we get:

ln(k) = ln(A) - (Ea / R) (1 / T)

This is in the form of a straight line, y = mx + c, where y = ln(k) and x = 1/T. Plotting ln(k) versus 1/T gives a straight line with a slope of -Ea/R and a y-intercept of ln(A).

2. Transition State Theory

Transition State Theory (TST) provides a more detailed, quantum-mechanical explanation of reaction rates. It proposes that reactants pass through a unique, high-energy intermediate state called the activated complex or transition state.

TST theorizes that for a reaction to proceed, the reactants must form an unstable, temporary species known as the transition state. This complex exists at the highest energy point on the reaction pathway and can either proceed to form products or revert back to reactants. The reaction rate is governed by the concentration of this activated complex and the rate at which it breaks down into products.

A reaction coordinate diagram is a graphical representation of the potential energy of a chemical system as a function of the progress of a reaction. The x-axis represents the reaction coordinate (the progress from reactants to products), and the y-axis represents the potential energy. The peak of the curve signifies the transition state. The energy difference between the reactants and this peak is the activation energy (Ea).

The transition state is a unique and fleeting species with several defining characteristics:

  • Extremely Unstable: It exists for an incredibly short period of time (10-15 to 10-14 seconds).
  • Partial Bonds: Bonds are simultaneously breaking and forming, a state often represented with dashed lines in chemical diagrams.
  • Hypothetical Species: It cannot be isolated or directly observed. Its structure and properties are inferred based on theoretical calculations and kinetic data.
  • Saddle Point: On a potential energy surface (a multi-dimensional version of the diagram above), the transition state exists at a "saddle point" – a maximum in one dimension (the reaction coordinate) and a minimum in all other dimensions.

The concept of a transition state is applicable to both types of reactions, but the energy relationship between reactants and products differs.

Spontaneous Reactions (Exergonic): In these reactions, the products have a lower Gibbs free energy (ΔG < 0) than the reactants. The transition state still represents an energy barrier (Ea) that must be overcome, but once past it, the reaction proceeds spontaneously, releasing energy.

Non-Spontaneous Reactions (Endergonic): Here, the products have a higher Gibbs free energy (ΔG > 0) than the reactants. The transition state is still the peak, but a continuous external input of energy is required for the reaction to proceed. The concept of the transition state still helps us understand the reaction pathway and the magnitude of the energy barrier.
