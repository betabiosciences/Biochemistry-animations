DNA Replication Models

1. Models of DNA Replication

Before 1958, scientists had three main hypotheses for how the DNA double helix could be copied. Each model proposed a different fate for the original "parent" DNA strands after replication.

The Conservative Model

This model proposed that the original parent double helix remained entirely intact after replication. A new, second double helix would be synthesized, consisting of two completely new strands.
Result: One "old" molecule and one "new" molecule.

The Semiconservative Model

This model, proposed by Watson and Crick, suggested that the parent double helix would unwind. Each parent strand would then serve as a template for a new complementary strand.
Result: Two "hybrid" molecules, each with one old strand and one new strand.

The Dispersive Model

This model was the most complex, suggesting that the parent double helix would be broken into fragments. These fragments would be copied and then reassembled, with the new and old DNA segments mixed together (dispersed) in both strands of the resulting molecules.
Result: Two "hybrid" molecules, with old and new DNA interspersed on both strands.

2. The Tool: Isopycnic Separation

To distinguish between the models, Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl needed a way to separate "old" DNA from "new" DNA. They couldn't just look at it; they had to use a physical property: density.

Isotopes and Density

DNA's building blocks contain nitrogen. They used two isotopes of nitrogen:
• ¹⁴N (light): The common, naturally occurring isotope.
• ¹⁵N (heavy): A heavier, non-radioactive isotope.

DNA synthesized with ¹⁵N is physically denser than DNA synthesized with ¹⁴N. This density difference, while tiny, is measurable.

Cesium Chloride (CsCl) Density Gradient

They used a technique called density gradient centrifugation.
1. A solution of Cesium Chloride (CsCl) is spun in an ultracentrifuge at extremely high speed for many hours.
2. The force causes the heavy CsCl molecules to form a stable density gradient, with the solution being most dense at the bottom of the tube and least dense at the top.
3. When DNA is added to this gradient and spun, it will migrate and settle at the point where its own density perfectly matches the density of the CsCl solution. This point is called its isopycnic point.

This method could precisely separate DNA into distinct bands:
• Heavy Band (¹⁵N-¹⁵N): Would settle low in the tube.
• Light Band (¹⁴N-¹⁴N): Would settle high in the tube.
• Hybrid Band (¹⁵N-¹⁴N): Would settle directly in the middle.

3. The Meselson-Stahl Experiment

Their experimental design was elegant in its simplicity.

  1. Grow "Heavy" Bacteria: They grew *E. coli* bacteria for many generations in a growth medium containing only the heavy ¹⁵N isotope. This ensured that 100% of the bacteria's DNA was "heavy" (¹⁵N-¹⁵N). This is Generation 0.
  2. The "Pulse": They rapidly transferred this entire colony of "heavy" bacteria into a new medium containing only the light ¹⁴N isotope. All *new* DNA synthesized from this moment on would have to be "light."
  3. Sample Collection: They collected samples of bacteria at precise intervals:
    • Immediately after transfer (Generation 0).
    • After ~20 minutes (Generation 1, time for one replication).
    • After ~40 minutes (Generation 2).
    • After ~60 minutes (Generation 3), and so on.
  4. Analysis: For each sample, they lysed the cells, extracted the DNA, and spun it in the CsCl density gradient to see where the DNA bands formed.

4. Expected Observations

Each model predicted a unique pattern of bands in the centrifuge tubes. (Gen 0 is 100% Heavy for all models).

If... Conservative Model is True:

  • Gen 1: Two bands. 50% Heavy (parent) and 50% Light (new).
  • Gen 2: Two bands. 25% Heavy and 75% Light.
  • Gen 3: Two bands. 12.5% Heavy and 87.5% Light.
  • Key: The original heavy band never disappears.

If... Semiconservative Model is True:

  • Gen 1: One band. 100% Hybrid density.
  • Gen 2: Two bands. 50% Hybrid and 50% Light.
  • Gen 3: Two bands. 25% Hybrid and 75% Light.
  • Key: The heavy band vanishes, replaced by hybrid.

If... Dispersive Model is True:

  • Gen 1: One band. 100% Hybrid (50% heavy, 50% light).
  • Gen 2: One band. Still 100% hybrid, but now only 25% heavy, 75% light. (The single band would *shift* upwards).
  • Gen 3: One band. Still 100% hybrid, but now 12.5% heavy, 87.5% light. (The band shifts even higher).
  • Key: Only one band ever appears, and it gets progressively lighter.

5. The Actual Results

By comparing their real results to the predictions, Meselson and Stahl could definitively solve the puzzle.

Observation: Generation 1

They observed a single, intermediate-density band (Hybrid). The original heavy band was gone.

Conclusion: The Conservative Model is Falsified.
(The conservative model predicted two bands: one heavy, one light).

Observation: Generation 2

They observed two distinct bands. One was at the hybrid density, and a new, second band appeared at the light (¹⁴N) density.

Conclusion: The Dispersive Model is Falsified.
(The dispersive model predicted only one band that would get lighter, not two distinct bands).

Final Verdict

The observed results—a single hybrid band in Generation 1, followed by one hybrid and one light band in Generation 2—perfectly matched the predictions of the Semiconservative Model.

This experiment unequivocally demonstrated that DNA replication is semiconservative.
