Protein Processing in Endoplasmic Reticulum

1. Glycosylation

N-linked Glycosylation Mechanism

N-linked glycosylation is a co-translational modification that occurs as proteins are synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum. This process begins with the assembly of a dolichol-linked oligosaccharide precursor on the cytosolic face of the ER membrane.

The core oligosaccharide (Glc₃Man₉GlcNAc₂) is assembled on a dolichol phosphate carrier through the sequential addition of monosaccharides by specific glycosyltransferases. Once the Man₅GlcNAc₂ intermediate is formed, the structure is flipped into the ER lumen where additional mannose and glucose residues are added.

The oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) complex recognizes the Asn-X-Ser/Thr consensus sequence (where X is any amino acid except proline) and catalyzes the en bloc transfer of the preassembled oligosaccharide to the asparagine residue of the nascent polypeptide chain.

Significance of N-linked Glycosylation

  • Protein folding: Glycans facilitate proper folding by recruiting chaperones
  • Quality control: Glycan processing serves as a timer for folding attempts
  • Intracellular trafficking: Glycans act as signals for protein localization
  • Stability: Glycans protect against proteolytic degradation
  • Cell-cell recognition: Surface glycoproteins mediate immune responses

2. Disulfide Bond Formation and Rearrangement

Oxidative Protein Folding Environment

The ER provides an oxidizing environment conducive to disulfide bond formation, contrasting with the reducing environment of the cytosol. This redox potential is maintained by a high ratio of oxidized to reduced glutathione and the action of ER oxidoreductases.

Protein Disulfide Isomerase (PDI) Family

The PDI family of enzymes catalyzes disulfide bond formation, reduction, and isomerization:

  • Active sites: Contain CXXC motifs with reactive cysteine residues
  • Mechanism: PDI transfers disulfide bonds to substrate proteins through thiol-disulfide exchange reactions
  • Chaperone activity: Many PDIs also facilitate protein folding independent of their redox function

ER Oxidoreductin 1 (Ero1) System

Ero1 proteins regenerate oxidized PDI by transferring electrons to molecular oxygen, forming hydrogen peroxide:

  • Ero1 contains conserved cysteine pairs that form regulatory disulfides
  • This system connects disulfide bond formation to cellular oxygen levels and metabolic state

Disulfide Bond Rearrangement

Initial disulfide bonds may not always be native, requiring rearrangement:

  • PDI catalyzes disulfide bond isomerization to correct non-native disulfides
  • This process is particularly important for proteins with multiple disulfide bonds
  • Rearrangement continues until the thermodynamically most stable configuration is achieved

Quality Control of Disulfide Bonds

The ER contains surveillance mechanisms to ensure proper disulfide bonding:

  • Persistent non-native disulfides target proteins for ERAD
  • Oxidoreductases work in concert with chaperones to monitor folding status

3. Protein Folding

Molecular Chaperones in the ER

The ER contains a specialized set of chaperones that facilitate proper protein folding by preventing aggregation, promoting correct folding pathways, and participating in quality control mechanisms.

BiP (Binding immunoglobulin Protein)

BiP is an Hsp70 family member that plays a central role in ER protein folding:

  • Substrate recognition: Binds hydrophobic regions exposed on unfolded or misfolded proteins
  • ATP-dependent mechanism: Cycles between ATP-bound (low affinity) and ADP-bound (high affinity) states
  • Co-chaperones: stimulate ATP hydrolysis and substrate binding
  • Nucleotide exchange: factors promote ADP release, resetting the chaperone cycle

BiP Mechanism of Action:

  1. ATP-bound BiP has low affinity for substrates
  2. ERdj co-chaperones deliver unfolded proteins and stimulate ATP hydrolysis
  3. ADP-bound BiP tightly binds hydrophobic regions of substrates
  4. Nucleotide exchange factors promote ADP release and ATP rebinding
  5. ATP binding causes conformational change and substrate release

Calnexin and Calreticulin

These lectin chaperones specifically recognize and assist glycoproteins:

  • Substrate recognition: Bind monoglucosylated N-glycans (Glc₁Man₉GlcNAc₂)
  • Membrane association: Calnexin is transmembrane; calreticulin is soluble

Chaperonins in the ER

While classic chaperonins like GroEL/GroES are not present in the ER, specialized complexes perform analogous functions:

Mechanism of Chaperonin-Assisted Folding

Chaperonins provide an isolated environment for protein folding:

Chaperonin Folding Cycle:

  1. Unfolded protein substrate binds to the open chaperonin chamber
  2. ATP binding triggers conformational changes and chamber closure
  3. Folding occurs in the isolated, hydrophilic environment
  4. ATP hydrolysis promotes chamber reopening
  5. Folded protein is released; misfolded proteins may undergo additional cycles

Folding Cycles and Attempts

Proteins undergo multiple folding attempts in the ER:

  • Chaperone binding prevents aggregation and promotes productive folding
  • ATP hydrolysis drives conformational changes in chaperone-substrate complexes
  • The calnexin/calreticulin cycle provides multiple folding attempts for glycoproteins
  • UGGT (UDP-glucose:glycoprotein glucosyltransferase) acts as a folding sensor, reglucosylating misfolded proteins for another round of chaperone binding

Collaborative Folding Networks

ER chaperones work in coordinated networks to ensure efficient protein folding:

  • Sequential assistance: Different chaperones act at specific stages of folding
  • Substrate transfer: Proteins may be passed between chaperones during folding
  • Quality control integration: Chaperones collaborate with ERAD machinery to identify terminally misfolded proteins

4. Cis/Trans Isomerization

Peptidyl-Prolyl Isomerases (PPIases)

PPIases catalyze the interconversion between cis and trans isomers of peptide bonds preceding proline residues.

Kinetic Trapping in Protein Folding

Proline isomerization can be a rate-limiting step in protein folding:

  • Non-native proline isomers can kinetically trap folding intermediates
  • Spontaneous isomerization is slow (half-times of minutes) at physiological temperatures
  • PPIases accelerate this process by up to 10⁶-fold, preventing kinetic traps

Biological Significance

Proline isomerization plays critical roles in:

  • Folding efficiency: Accelerating the folding of proline-rich proteins
  • Regulation: Acting as molecular switches in signaling pathways
  • Disease: Mutations affecting proline isomerization are linked to various pathologies
