Protein Translation: Elongation Stage

1. Overview of Translation Elongation

The elongation stage is the central phase where the polypeptide chain is extended by sequentially adding amino acids. This process requires precise coordination between the ribosome, mRNA, transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and various elongation factors.

Peptidyl Transferase

Peptidyl transferase is the enzymatic activity responsible for catalyzing the formation of peptide bonds between amino acids. This activity resides in the large ribosomal subunit (50S in prokaryotes, 60S in eukaryotes) and is fundamentally a ribozyme - an RNA enzyme. The catalytic center consists primarily of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), with proteins playing a supportive structural role.

The mechanism involves deprotonation of the amino group of the aminoacyl-tRNA in the A site, making it nucleophilic enough to attack the carbonyl carbon of the peptidyl-tRNA in the P site. This results in the transfer of the growing polypeptide chain to the aminoacyl-tRNA in the A site, extending the chain by one amino acid.

Translocation

Translocation is the coordinated movement of the ribosome relative to the mRNA and tRNAs after peptide bond formation. This process shifts the tRNAs from the A (aminoacyl) and P (peptidyl) sites to the P and E (exit) sites respectively, making the A site available for the next aminoacyl-tRNA.

Translocation is catalyzed by elongation factor G (EF-G) in prokaryotes and elongation factor 2 (eEF2) in eukaryotes. These factors hydrolyze GTP to provide the energy needed for the conformational changes in the ribosome that drive translocation. The process ensures that the mRNA moves exactly three nucleotides (one codon) through the ribosome, maintaining the correct reading frame.

2. Elongation in Prokaryotes

In prokaryotes, elongation is a highly coordinated process involving specific elongation factors and precise ribosomal movements. The process can be divided into three main steps: decoding, peptide bond formation, and translocation.

Step-by-Step Mechanism:

  1. Aminoacyl-tRNA Delivery: Elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu) in complex with GTP binds to aminoacyl-tRNA, forming a ternary complex. This complex delivers the aminoacyl-tRNA to the A site of the ribosome. The correct codon-anticodon pairing triggers GTP hydrolysis by EF-Tu, leading to its release from the ribosome.
  2. Accommodation: After EF-Tu release, the aminoacyl-tRNA moves into the correct position in the A site for peptide bond formation. This movement, called accommodation, involves a conformational change in the tRNA from an A/T state (where it's bound to both the A site and EF-Tu) to the fully accommodated A/A state.
  3. Peptide Bond Formation: The peptidyl transferase center catalyzes the nucleophilic attack of the amino group of the A-site aminoacyl-tRNA on the carbonyl carbon of the P-site peptidyl-tRNA. This transfers the growing polypeptide chain to the A-site tRNA, extending it by one amino acid.
  4. Translocation: Elongation factor G (EF-G) in complex with GTP binds to the ribosome and catalyzes translocation. This moves the peptidyl-tRNA from the A site to the P site, the deacylated tRNA from the P site to the E site, and shifts the mRNA by exactly one codon.

Ribosome Ratcheting:

Ribosome ratcheting refers to the rotational movement of the small ribosomal subunit relative to the large subunit by approximately 6-10 degrees during translocation. This movement is essential for moving tRNAs between binding sites and advancing the mRNA through the ribosome.

The ratcheting motion is coupled to the GTP hydrolysis by EF-G. The energy from GTP hydrolysis induces conformational changes in EF-G that physically push against the ribosome, forcing the rotational movement. After ratcheting, the ribosome spontaneously returns to the non-rotated state, completing the translocation cycle.

Ribosome ratcheting facilitates the movement of tRNA molecules by altering the geometry of their binding sites. In the rotated state, the A and P sites adopt hybrid configurations (A/P and P/E), where the anticodon ends remain associated with the small subunit while the acceptor ends move to interact with the large subunit sites normally associated with the next position.

3. Mechanisms to Increase Fidelity

The ribosome employs multiple sophisticated mechanisms to ensure accurate translation with remarkably low error rates, typically around 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ per codon. These fidelity mechanisms operate at different stages of the elongation cycle.

Key Fidelity Mechanisms:

  • Kinetic Proofreading: This two-step selection process significantly enhances discrimination between correct and incorrect tRNAs. The initial selection occurs when the aminoacyl-tRNA-EF-Tu complex binds to the ribosome. Correct codon-anticodon pairing increases the residence time, allowing GTP hydrolysis. After GTP hydrolysis, a second discrimination step occurs during accommodation, where incorrect tRNAs are more likely to dissociate before peptide bond formation.
  • Induced Fit: Correct codon-anticodon pairing induces conformational changes in the ribosome that stabilize tRNA binding. Specifically, conserved nucleotides in the decoding center of the small ribosomal subunit (such as A1492, A1493, and G530 in E. coli) monitor the geometry of the codon-anticodon helix. When correct Watson-Crick base pairing occurs, these nucleotides undergo conformational changes that close around the codon-anticodon duplex, stabilizing the interaction and promoting GTP hydrolysis by EF-Tu.
  • EF-Tu GTPase Activation: EF-Tu's GTPase activity is specifically activated by correct codon-anticodon pairing. The conformational changes in the decoding center triggered by correct pairing are transmitted to EF-Tu, accelerating GTP hydrolysis. For incorrect pairings, this activation is less efficient, allowing more time for the incorrect tRNA to dissociate before GTP hydrolysis commits it to accommodation.

These mechanisms work synergistically to achieve the remarkable accuracy of protein synthesis. The energy cost of this fidelity is the hydrolysis of GTP by EF-Tu and EF-G for each amino acid incorporated, making translation an energy-intensive process but ensuring the production of functional proteins.

4. Elongation in Eukaryotes

Eukaryotic elongation shares fundamental mechanisms with prokaryotes but involves more complex regulation and different elongation factors. The basic steps of decoding, peptide bond formation, and translocation are conserved, but the specific factors and regulatory mechanisms differ significantly.

Step-by-Step Mechanism:

  1. eEF1A Delivery: Eukaryotic elongation factor 1A (eEF1A) delivers aminoacyl-tRNA to the A site in a GTP-dependent manner. eEF1A is functionally analogous to prokaryotic EF-Tu but has a different structure and regulation. Correct codon-anticodon pairing triggers GTP hydrolysis by eEF1A.
  2. eEF1B-Mediated Recycling: Eukaryotic elongation factor 1B (eEF1B) acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for eEF1A, catalyzing the exchange of GDP for GTP. This recycles eEF1A to its active form for subsequent rounds of elongation.
  3. eEF2-Mediated Translocation: Eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (eEF2) promotes translocation in a GTP-dependent manner. eEF2 binds to the ribosome and induces conformational changes that move the mRNA and tRNAs. After translocation, eEF2-GDP dissociates from the ribosome.

5. Comparison of Elongation Factors

Elongation factors are essential proteins that facilitate the translation elongation process. While prokaryotes and eukaryotes share similar elongation mechanisms, their elongation factors have distinct properties and regulatory mechanisms. The table below provides a comprehensive comparison:

Function Prokaryotic Factor Eukaryotic Factor
Aminoacyl-tRNA delivery to A site EF-Tu (Elongation Factor Thermo unstable) eEF1A (Eukaryotic Elongation Factor 1A)
GTP recycling for aminoacyl-tRNA delivery factor EF-Ts (Elongation Factor Thermo stable) eEF1B (Eukaryotic Elongation Factor 1B)
Translocation EF-G (Elongation Factor G) eEF2 (Eukaryotic Elongation Factor 2)
Ribosome binding specificity Binds specifically to 70S ribosomes Binds specifically to 80S ribosomes

Evolutionary Conservation and Divergence

Despite significant structural and regulatory differences, elongation factors share a common evolutionary origin. EF-Tu/eEF1A and EF-G/eEF2 belong to the GTPase superfamily and contain conserved structural domains responsible for GTP binding and hydrolysis. The core mechanisms of tRNA delivery and ribosome translocation have been conserved throughout evolution, while regulatory mechanisms have diversified to meet the specific needs of different organisms.
