Prokaryotic Transcription: Elongation Stage

Elongation Factors

During transcription elongation, RNA polymerase is assisted by various elongation factors that modulate its activity, enhance processivity, and facilitate proofreading.

NusG Transcription-Translation Coupling

NusG plays a critical role in coupling transcription with translation in prokaryotes. It binds to RNA polymerase and the ribosome, ensuring that translation closely follows transcription. This coupling is essential for maintaining transcription processivity and preventing premature termination.

NusA Transcription Pausing and Termination

NusA modulates transcription pausing and termination. It binds to RNA polymerase and enhances its ability to recognize pause and termination signals. NusA also participates in antitermination complexes that allow RNA polymerase to read through certain termination signals.

NusE Ribosomal Protein S10

NusE is actually the ribosomal protein S10, which participates in transcription antitermination complexes. It helps form the "Nus" complex that prevents premature termination at specific sites, particularly in rRNA operons.

GreA Transcript Cleavage Factor

GreA is involved in transcript cleavage during elongation. It assists RNA polymerase in backtracking and cleaving the nascent RNA when the polymerase encounters obstacles, facilitating transcription restart.

GreB Transcript Cleavage and Proofreading

GreB stimulates the endonucleolytic cleavage activity of RNA polymerase. It helps resolve backtracked transcription complexes by promoting cleavage of the 3' end of the nascent RNA, which is crucial for proofreading and recovery from transcriptional arrest.

Proofreading by RNA Polymerase

RNA polymerase employs multiple proofreading mechanisms to ensure transcription fidelity despite lacking the intrinsic 3'→5' exonuclease activity of DNA polymerases.

Kinetic Proofreading (Pyrophosphorolytic Editing)

This proofreading mechanism relies on the difference in kinetics between correct and incorrect nucleotide incorporation. When an incorrect nucleotide is incorporated, the elongation complex becomes less stable and tends to slide backward.

Pyrophosphorolytic editing involves the reverse reaction of nucleotide addition. RNA polymerase can use pyrophosphate (PPi) to remove incorrectly incorporated nucleotides through the reverse of the polymerization reaction: RNA(n) + NTP ⇌ RNA(n+1) + PPi.

This mechanism is particularly effective when the concentration of PPi is relatively high, favoring the reverse reaction and excision of misincorporated nucleotides.

Nucleolytic Proofreading (Hydrolytic Editing)

Hydrolytic editing involves the direct cleavage of the RNA transcript. When RNA polymerase incorporates an incorrect nucleotide, it may backtrack, displacing the 3' end of the RNA from the active site.

In this backtracked state, the polymerase can cleave the RNA transcript, removing the misincorporated nucleotide and several adjacent nucleotides. This cleavage reaction is stimulated by transcription factors like GreA and GreB.

After cleavage, RNA polymerase can resume transcription from the newly generated 3' end, having effectively corrected the error. This mechanism is particularly important for recovering from prolonged transcriptional pauses.

RNA Polymerase Movement During Elongation

RNA polymerase does not move smoothly along the DNA template during elongation. Instead, it exhibits a "stuttering" movement characterized by periods of rapid elongation interspersed with pauses and backtracking.

Stuttering Mechanism

The stuttering movement of RNA polymerase serves several important functions:

Pausing: RNA polymerase frequently pauses during elongation. These pauses can be:

  • Elementary pauses (lasting milliseconds)
  • Backtracked pauses (lasting seconds to minutes)
  • Regulatory pauses (at specific DNA sequences)

Backtracking: During backtracking, RNA polymerase moves backward along the DNA template while maintaining its contact with the transcript. This displaces the 3' end of the RNA from the active site, temporarily halting elongation.

Functional Significance: Stuttering allows for:

  • Proofreading and error correction
  • Coordination with other cellular processes
  • Regulation of gene expression through pausing
  • Facilitation of transcription-coupled repair

The stuttering behavior is influenced by DNA sequence, transcription factors, and the cellular environment, making it a regulated aspect of transcription elongation rather than a simple byproduct of the mechanism.
