Fundamentals of Absorption

When light passes through a sample, some energies are taken up by the sample’s particles. Each allowed “jump” creates a dip in transmitted light at a specific wavelength—this pattern is a spectrum.

Bonding vs antibonding

Bonding orbitals (σ, π)
Electron density lies between nuclei, pulling atoms together. Fewer nodes (regions of zero electron density). Lower energy, stabilizing.
Antibonding orbitals (σ*, π*)
A node sits between nuclei, pushing atoms apart. More nodes. Higher energy, destabilizing. Occupying these weakens the bond.
Non‑bonding (n)
Lone pairs that are mostly on one atom. They often jump to π* or σ* in the UV region.
Selection tips
  • σ → σ*: needs the most energy (far UV).
  • π → π*: strong, moves to longer λ with more conjugation.
  • n → π*: moderate, often gives pale colors.
  • n → σ*: UV, common in saturated groups with heteroatoms.

UV–Visible Absorption

UV–Vis probes electron jumps between orbitals. Conjugation (linked double bonds) narrows the energy gap, shifting peaks to longer wavelengths (red shift). Substituents and solvent can fine‑tune both peak position and intensity.

  • π → π* in conjugated systems is typically strong and can enter the visible region.
  • n → π* transitions are weaker (often pale) and sensitive to environment (e.g., hydrogen bonding).
  • Broader peaks can come from many closely spaced vibronic levels in solution.

Practical notes

  • Keep absorbance in a sensible range (roughly 0.1–1.5) for best linearity.
  • Use quartz cuvettes for measurements below ~320 nm (glass absorbs UV).
  • Baseline with pure solvent; match cuvette orientation to reduce stray differences.

Beer–Lambert Law

The law links absorbance (A) to how much absorbing species you have and how far light travels: A = ε × c × l.

Interactive calculator

Tip: If A is above ~1.5, too little light gets through—dilute your sample for better accuracy.

What each term means

  • A (absorbance): a measure of light loss; unitless. A = −log₁₀(T), where T is transmittance.
  • ε (molar absorptivity): how strongly a substance absorbs at a given wavelength; units M⁻¹·cm⁻¹. Depends on the transition and λ.
  • c (concentration): amount of substance per volume. Choose consistent units; here M (mol/L) is used with ε.
  • l (path length): how far light travels through the sample, usually 1.00 cm in standard cuvettes.
Limitations: high concentrations can scatter light or change refractive index; chemical equilibria can shift with concentration or solvent; stray light flattens curves at high A.
